13 · Implied and reduced conditionals

A conditional argument without “if.”

ACCESS

Goal. Read the memo excerpt and notice the moves in bold. You are spotting the pattern here, not producing it yet.

Legal Can we move forward without the client's signature?

Priya (ops) Not really. Without their sign-off, we can't start the migration. Otherwise, we'd be working from an unapproved plan.

Legal What if they push back on the timeline?

Priya Given their history, that seems unlikely. Assuming no objections, we start Monday.

Legal Fine, let's proceed on that basis.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how to make a conditional argument using prepositions and participles instead of "if," for a leaner, more formal register.

The structure

Not every condition needs the word if. Formal and professional English often implies a condition through a preposition, a participle, or a stand-alone connector, which reads as tighter and more official than a full if-clause. Without X, Y implies if X does not happen. Given X, Y implies if we accept X as true. Assuming X, Y and Otherwise, Y both point back to an unstated condition the reader is expected to infer.

Preposition-led conditionals

Form
without / given / with + noun phrase, main clause
Meaning
an implied condition, stated as a fact rather than a full if-clause
Use it to
state a condition compactly, in a formal or official register
Without their sign-off, we can’t start the migration. Given their history, that seems unlikely.

Assuming / otherwise

Form
assuming + clause, main clause; main clause. Otherwise, consequence
Meaning
a condition implied rather than stated with "if"
Use it to
reference an unstated condition the reader can infer from context
Assuming no objections, we start Monday. Otherwise, we’d be working from an unapproved plan.

Watch out

Don't stack "if" with these connectors. "If without their sign-off" doubles the conditional marker. Use one or the other: "without their sign-off" alone, or "if they don't sign off."

Make sure "otherwise" has a clear antecedent. "Otherwise" only works if the condition it reverses was stated or is obvious from context. If it isn't, spell out the condition instead.

American / British English.

"Provided that" versus "provided." British formal English often drops "that" after "provided" in contracts: "provided the terms are met." American formal English usually keeps "that": "provided that the terms are met." This book keeps "that."

The moves

Same implied logic in every line. The highlighted slot changes; no "if" appears anywhere.

  • Without [their sign-off / a firm date], we can't start.
  • Given [their history / the current data], that seems unlikely.
  • Assuming [no objections / normal conditions], we start Monday.
  • With [more time / another engineer], we could finish early.
  • Otherwise, we'd be working from [an unapproved plan / an outdated spec].

Where each move sits

The same condition, restated without the word if, for a tighter and more official register.

Why this works

Formal documents reward density, and repeating "if" across a paragraph of conditions starts to sound like a checklist rather than an argument. Implied conditionals let you state the same logic in fewer, more official-sounding words: "without X" and "given X" both smuggle a condition into a noun phrase instead of a clause. The trade-off is that the reader has to do a small amount of inference to recover the "if," so these forms work best when the condition is genuinely easy to reconstruct from context, not when the logic is delicate or easy to misread.
