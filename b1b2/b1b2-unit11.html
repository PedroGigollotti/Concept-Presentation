Second conditional: imaginary situations

Talking about what is not true, and what you would do about it.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn the tense for what is not true. By the end, you can imagine a different situation, and give advice without standing on the person you give it to.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Could this actually happen?

If yes, use unit 10: if + present, will. If no, use this unit: if + past form, would. Ask this before you choose.

THE FORM

the conditionif + past formif I had more time
the resultwould or 'd + base verbI would travel
the verb bewere, for every personif I were you

A past form that is not about the past

Look at what Caio said. “If I were you, I would take it.”

Were is a past form. Nothing in that sentence is about the past. He is talking about right now, and about a Caio who does not exist.

That is the trick, and English uses it on purpose. The form steps back one place. The time does not move at all. The gap between them is the signal, and the signal means: THIS IS NOT TRUE.

So the past form here is not a tense. It is a marker. It tells your listener not to search for this in the real world.

Real, or imaginary

This is the question that chooses between two units, and it is worth asking out loud.

Unit 9 was for things that CAN happen. If it rains, we will cancel. Rain is a genuine possibility, so the tenses stay ordinary: present, then will.

This unit is for things that are NOT true, or are very unlikely. If I won a million, I would travel. Both halves step back: past form, then would.

Nothing else changes. Same two halves, same comma rule, same either order. Only the tenses move, and they move together.

Were, for everybody

One small thing surprises people. With the verb be, this form uses were for every person: if I were, if he were, if it were.

You will hear if I was from native speakers, and it is common in speech. In writing, and in anything that has to sound considered, use were.

If I were you is the phrase you will use most, and it never changes. Careful English keeps were there.

Advice you can refuse

This is where the choice becomes yours, and it is why the form is worth having.

“Take the job” is an order. “You should take the job” is a judgment.

“If I were you, I would take the job” is neither. It puts the advice in an imaginary person's mouth, and it hands the decision back.

Use it when the decision is genuinely the other person's and you still want to say what you think. That is most of the time.

The form steps back, the time does not

The idea
The condition takes a past form and the result takes would. Neither of them is about the past. The gap between the form and the time is what says the whole thing is not true.

from your own week
if I had more time · if we knew the budget · if he spoke French
If I had more time, I would learn Spanish.

Advice you can refuse

The idea
This part is your choice. The form puts your advice in an imaginary person's mouth. It arrives as something to weigh, not something to obey.
a judgment
You should take the job.
something she can weigh
If I were you, I would take the job.
Same advice. Only one of them leaves the decision hers.

Watch out

No would in the if half. Say “if I had more time,” never “if I would have more time.” The two halves take different things and they never swap.

Would takes the base verb. Say “I would take it,” never “I would to take it.” Your own language puts an infinitive there and English does not.

The moves

The frame stays the same. You change only the blue words. The column you pick decides whether you tell the other person or ask him.

Something she can weigh

  • If I were you, I would [take it].
  • If I had [the choice], I would [wait].
  • What would you do if [the money were the same]?

Something she is told

  • You should [take it].
  • I would [wait], in your position.
  • [Take] the job.

The verb moves back a step. The clock does not move at all, and that gap is the signal.

Why this works

Most of the useful things you say about a decision are about a world that does not exist. What if the budget were bigger. What if we had another week. English gives you one small move for all of it: step the verb back, and everybody knows you are imagining. It also gives you the politest way there is to tell somebody what to do. You describe what you would do instead.
