Participle clauses

Saying two things in the space of one.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn to shorten one half of a sentence to a participle. By the end, you can compress two clauses into one, and know whose action the short half is.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Whose action is the -ing? It has to be the subject of the other half.

The short half has no subject and cannot get one. It borrows from the main half, every time. Ask this before you shorten anything.

THE FORM

the long formas / while / because + subject + verbas I arrived at seven
shortened, active-ing, and no subjectarriving at seven
shortened, passive-ed participle, and no subjectwritten in a hurry
what happened firsthaving + participlehaving checked the racks

The long form it comes from

Before the short one, the long one. English joins two halves with as, while or because.

As and while put two things at the same time. “As I arrived at seven, I found the door open.”

As has a second job that while does not: it can mean because. “As nobody answered, I called security.”

That is worth noticing, because the same three letters do two different jobs and only the sense separates them.

Everything in the rest of this unit is one of these sentences with the first half cut short.

Cut it short, and it loses its subject

“As I arrived at seven” becomes “arriving at seven”. The as goes, the I goes, and the verb takes -ing.

That missing subject is the entire rule of this unit. The short half has none and cannot get one, so it borrows from the other half.

“Arriving at seven, I found the server room open.” The person arriving is the person finding, because there is nobody else on offer.

So the test is short. Before you shorten a half, check that both halves have the same subject. If they do not, leave the long form alone.

That is not a style rule. It is the difference between reporting what you did and reporting what somebody else did.

Active takes -ing, passive takes the participle

If the subject is doing the action, the short half takes -ing. “Arriving at seven…” He arrived.

If the action is being done TO the subject, it takes the past participle instead. “Written in a hurry, the report had errors.” Nobody wrote the report in that half; the report was written.

It is unit 31's distinction with the be removed. Being written becomes just written.

And having plus a participle puts one thing before the other. “Having checked the racks, I called security.” The checking finished first.

Three shapes, then. -ing for at the same time, -ed for done to it, having for before.

Seeing the whole thing, or catching it mid-way

A participle also turns up after certain verbs of noticing, and there it makes a distinction worth having.

See, hear, watch, notice, feel. Each of them takes an object and then either a bare verb or an -ing.

“The cleaner saw somebody leave” means she watched the whole departure, start to finish.

“The cleaner saw somebody leaving” means she caught part of it. The person was mid-way out.

That is the same whole-against-in-progress contrast you have had since unit 4, in a shape nobody points out.

What the compression is for, and what it costs

This is where the choice becomes yours, and both versions say the same thing.

The long form is the spoken one. As I arrived at seven, I found the door open. Nobody will blink at it.

The short form is the written one. It is tighter, it moves faster, and a report full of as and while reads as slow.

What it costs is a subject. In a long paragraph of participles, the reader is holding the subject in his head across every one of them.

So compress where the subject is obvious and stable, and go back to the long form the moment it changes.

Watch out

After having, the participle. Say “having checked the racks,” never “having check the racks” and never “having wrote the report.” The irregular list from unit 5 is still the list.

A verb of noticing takes a bare verb, not to. Say “she saw somebody leave,” never “she saw somebody to leave.” The to has no place after see, hear or watch.

The short half takes whatever subject the other half has, and it will take a wrong one. “Walking into the room, the lights were off” breaks no rule of form. It says the lights walked in. English will hand the action to whatever subject is standing there, so check which one that is before you shorten anything.

The moves

The frame stays the same. You change only the blue words. The column you pick decides whether the short half is doing the action or having it done to it.

The subject does it

  • [Arriving] at seven, I found the door open.
  • [Walking] into the room, I noticed the lights.
  • [Having] checked the racks, I called security.

It is done to the subject

  • [Written] in a hurry, the report had errors.
  • [Sent] without a check, it reached the client.
  • [Signed] that morning, the contract was already out of date.

One red chip has gone. The arrow is the short half reaching back for the one that is left.

Why this works

Written English compresses, and this is the commonest way it does it. A report full of as and while reads slowly, and the same content in participles reads as though somebody edited it. The care it needs is in one place only. The short half has no subject, so it takes whichever one is standing next to it. It will take a wrong one without complaining. Check that before you shorten, and the rest of this is free.

F

Over to you

No checking here. This one is yours.

In pairs. Tell the story of a morning that went wrong, in six sentences, all of them long form with as and while. Then tell it again with every first half cut short. Ask your partner whether the second version still says who did what.

On your own. Take a paragraph you have actually written at work and shorten two of its halves to participles. Then read it back and check, for each one, that the subject standing next to it is the person you meant.
