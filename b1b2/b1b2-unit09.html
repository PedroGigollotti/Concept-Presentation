Few, little, enough, and plenty of

Saying how much there is, and whether it will do.

ACCESS

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn to measure an amount precisely. By the end, you can say exactly how much there is, and decide whether that sounds like almost enough or almost nothing.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Is there an a in front of it?

A few and a little mean SOME, and they sound positive. Few and little mean ALMOST NONE, and they sound negative. One small word, and the verdict flips.

THE FORM

things you can counta few · fewa few testers
things you cannot counta little · littlea little time

The smallest word in the unit does the most work

Look at what Nuno said. “We have a few testers.” And then: “We have little time.”

Both sentences report a small amount. One of them sounds fine and one of them sounds like a problem. The only difference is the a.

A few and a little mean SOME. They point at what you have. Three testers, and three is workable.

Few and little mean ALMOST NONE. They point at what is missing. Two days, and two is not workable.

So the amount does not change and the verdict does. That is unusual in a language, and it is why this pair is worth an hour.

Which of the two you use, and it is about counting

A few and few go with things you can count: testers, days, emails, people.

A little and little go with things you cannot: time, money, space, help.

You already know which is which. Ask whether you could put a number in front of it. Three testers works, so testers count. Three times works too, but three time does not, so time does not count.

Enough, plenty of and a lot of take both kinds and never change shape. Plenty of and a lot of are the same amount; the second one is the one you say out loud. That is the easy end of this unit.

The nouns you cannot count

Unit 9 asked whether you can count a thing. For a small group of common nouns the answer is never, and they are worth learning as a list.

Advice, information, news, research, equipment, software, furniture, progress.

None of them takes a plural. None of them takes a. You cannot say an advice and you cannot say three informations.

When you need to count them, you count a container instead.

A piece of advice. Two pieces of information. A bit of research.

News is the one that catches people. It ends in s and it is singular. “The news is good.”

Enough is a verdict, not an amount

Enough says nothing about size. It says the size matches the job.

Two testers can be enough and twenty can fail to be. The word is measuring the amount against what you need, and nothing else.

That is also why it moves. Before a noun it goes in front: enough days. After an adjective it goes behind: fast enough, good enough.

Almost enough, or almost nothing

This is where the choice becomes yours, and it is the whole reason this pair exists.

Two days is a fact. We have a little time and we have little time are both true about those two days.

The first one buys you the room to try. The second one asks for help, or for a new date.

Choose the first when you intend to deliver and the second when you need somebody to act. Choosing the wrong one is how a team ends up surprised on Thursday.

Almost enough, or almost nothing

The idea

Watch out

Little and few do not swap. Say “a little time,” never “a little days.” Ask whether a number fits in front of the noun, and let that choose.

Enough before a noun, behind an adjective. Say “fast enough,” never “enough fast.” It is the one word in this unit that moves, and it moves for that one reason.

These nouns take no plural and no a. Say “they gave us advice,” never “they gave us advices” and never “an advice.” The same goes for information, news and research.

The moves

The frame stays the same. You change only the blue words. The column you pick decides whether the reader relaxes or reaches for the phone.

Almost enough

  • We have a few [testers].
  • There is a little [time] left.
  • We have enough [people] for this.

Almost nothing

  • We have few [testers].
  • There is little [time] left.
  • We do not have enough [people] for this.

Few and a few are for things you can count: a few emails. Little and a little are for things you cannot: a little time.

Why this works

Every plan comes down to whether what you have will do. Most languages answer that with a number and a shrug. English gives you a word that carries the number and the verdict at once. It hides the verdict in a single letter. Get that letter right and nobody has to ask you what you meant.
