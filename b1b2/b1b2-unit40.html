ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn what a word takes with it. By the end, you can store a new word with its company instead of on its own.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Which words does this word travel with?

Four things travel with a word. The verb it takes, the preposition it takes, the pattern that follows it, and the order it sits in. None can be derived. All of them can be learned together.

The last thing between correct and native

You now have the grammar of this level. This unit is about the layer above it, and it is the one people notice.

Do a decision breaks no rule you have learned. Every word is real, the tense is right, the order is right. It is simply not what English says.

That is collocation: which words travel together. It cannot be derived, only met and stored.

Every bank in this book has been teaching it quietly for thirty-nine units.

That is why the entries were expressions rather than single words.

This unit names the four kinds of company a word keeps. From here you can store a new word properly on the first meeting.

The verb a noun takes

English has a small set of verbs that do almost nothing on their own and carry enormous numbers of nouns.

You make a decision, an offer, a case, a mistake, progress, an effort. You do research, business, a favor, the work, your best.

You take a view, a break, responsibility, a risk. You give a talk, an answer, an example.

There is no rule behind any of that, and a search for one wastes the hour. The pairing belongs to the noun, so learn the noun with its verb attached.

Make a decision is one item to remember, not two.

The preposition a word takes

The second kind of company, and the one this reader gets wrong most.

Depend on. Rely on. Insist on. Good at. Bad at. Interested in. Succeed in. Afraid of. Capable of. Similar to. Responsible for.

The preposition is not chosen by meaning; it belongs to the word. That is why responsible for and in charge of mean nearly the same and take different prepositions.

Portuguese chooses differently, and the differences are not systematic. Depender de gives you depend of, which is not English.

So store the preposition inside the word. Not depend, but depend on.

The pattern that follows

The third kind, and this one changes meaning rather than merely being right or wrong.

“I am afraid of sounding obstructive” is a worry about a possible result.

“I am afraid to say it” is a refusal. He is not doing it, because he is afraid.

Other adjectives take to and a verb without any choice at all. Ready to go. Happy to help. Difficult to explain. Likely to fail.

And a few nouns carry a fixed frame of their own. There is no point in arguing. There is no use complaining. Learn those whole.

The order adjectives sit in

The fourth kind is an order, and almost no native speaker knows it consciously.

Opinion comes first, then size, then age, then shape, then color, then origin, then material, then what it is for.

A short, clear, written note. Opinion, size, material. Move any of them and it stops sounding English.

You will never apply this list in real time, and you do not need to. Two or three adjectives is the practical maximum, and the order is nearly always obvious once you hear it.

Keep it as a check for writing rather than a rule for speaking.

Precision, and the cost of the near miss

This is where the choice becomes yours, and it is the closing choice of the book.

Raise a concern and make a complaint are not the same act. One opens a conversation and the other opens a file.

Meet a standard and approach a standard differ by everything that matters.

The near miss is the expensive one. A reader forgives a wrong tense because he can see what you meant.

He cannot see it when the pairing is wrong, because the sentence still means something.

So when a pairing matters, check it rather than guess. That habit is worth more than any rule in this book.

Where to go from here

One last thing, and then the book is done.

You have the grammar. What separates you from a native speaker now is not structure; it is company, and company is collected rather than studied.

Every time you meet a word that matters to your work, write down what it traveled with. The verb, the preposition, the pattern.

Do that for a year and you will have something no course can give you.

The pairings of your own field, in your own words.

That is the whole method, and it is the only part of it you have to do alone.

Watch out

The preposition belongs to the word, and it is not the Portuguese one. Say “it depends on the cost,” never “it depends of the cost.” And say “good at it,” never “good in it.”

Make and do are not interchangeable. Say “make a decision” and “do research,” never “do a decision” or “make a research.” No rule decides it. The noun does.

Afraid of doing and afraid to do are both correct English. They mean different things. Afraid of sounding rude is a worry about a result. Afraid to say it is a refusal to act. Choosing the wrong one does not sound wrong; it says something you did not mean.

AMERICAN AND BRITISH ENGLISH

Collocation is where the two Englishes disagree most quietly, because nothing is ever wrong enough to notice.

British: different from or different to · in the team · write to me · fill in a form.

American: different from or different than · on the team · write me · fill out a form.

Different from is safe in both, which makes it the one to use when you do not want to think about it.

This book uses American English throughout, so the exercises take on the team and write me. Neither will be misunderstood anywhere.

The moves

The frame stays the same. You change only the blue words. The column you pick decides how precisely you are naming the act.

Opening a conversation

  • I want to [raise] a concern about the timing.
  • Let me [make] the case in writing.
  • We should [draw] a distinction between the two.

Naming a limit

  • It [depends] on the cost.
  • We have to [strike] a balance.
  • The draft does not [meet] the standard yet.

Nothing in the right-hand column explains which row it belongs to. That is the lesson.

Why this works

This is the last unit and it is about the last thing. You have the grammar of B2 now, and grammar is not what will mark you out. Nobody has ever been thought foreign for a tense. They are thought foreign for do a decision, which breaks no rule at all. The near miss is the expensive one. A wrong pairing still means something, and your reader has no way to see what you meant. From here the work changes shape. It stops being study and becomes collection, and it is the only part of this you have to do yourself.

This check looks at two things only: that your collocation is there and that it looks like a sentence. It CANNOT tell you whether the rest of your pairings are right, which is the whole subject of this unit and the one thing no checker will ever do for you. That is the point the unit ends on.

F

Over to you, and from here on it is all over to you

No checking here. This one is yours, and so is everything after it.

In pairs. Disagree with each other about something real at work, for five minutes, using raise a concern, make a case, draw a distinction and strike a balance at least once each. Then say the same things with the wrong verbs and listen to how little breaks and how much changes.

On your own. Take ten words that matter in your own job. For each one, write down what it travels with: the verb, the preposition, the pattern that follows. Ten lines. That is the first page of the only vocabulary book that will ever fit you.

And after that. Do the same thing once a week, for a year. You have the grammar of this level and nobody can give you the company. It is collected, one word at a time, in the language of the work you actually do.
