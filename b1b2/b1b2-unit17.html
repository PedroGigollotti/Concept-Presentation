Reported speech: statements and backshift

VOCABULARY

The verbs for saying HOW somebody said it. Each one is a claim about them.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn reported statements and the backshift. By the end, you can carry what somebody said to a third person, accurately, and choose a verb that is fair to them.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Is the reporting verb in the past?

If it is, everything after it steps back one notch. If it is not, nothing moves at all.

THE FORM

no listener namedsaid (that) + clauseshe said she was swamped
the listener namedtold + person + (that) + clauseshe told me she had finished
the step backpresent → past
will → would
can → could		I am busy → she said she was busy

Nothing in the step back is new

Look at what Amy does. Sara said I am swamped, and Amy reports she was swamped.

Every form the step back reaches for is one you already have. The past simple is unit 1. The past perfect is unit 5.

Will becomes would. You met that in unit 6 and again in unit 11. Can becomes could, from units 13 and 14.

So there is no new tense here, and nothing to memorize. There is one instruction: move each form you already own one notch further back.

The small words move with them. I becomes he or she. Now becomes then. Here becomes there.

Say needs nobody. Tell needs somebody

These two verbs are not interchangeable, and the rule is one line long.

Tell takes the listener immediately after it: she told me, he told the client. No to goes in between.

Say takes no listener at all. She said she was swamped. If you want to name the listener with say, you cannot.

So the choice is made for you by whether the listener is in the sentence. Name him and you need tell. Leave him out and you need say.

The step back is a default, not a law

Here is the part most books leave out, and you will hear it constantly.

When what was said is STILL TRUE, English speakers often leave the tense where it was. She said she is Brazilian. He said the office opens at eight.

Both of those are correct, and so are the shifted versions. The shift is normal, safe, and always available. It is not obligatory.

Where it does matter is the opposite case. If the thing is over, shifting back is the only honest option. Leaving it forward claims it is still true.

So use the step back as your default. Just do not be confused when a colleague does not, because he has not made a mistake.

The verb you choose puts words in their mouth

This is where reporting stops being grammar, and it is the part that can cost somebody else.

He mentioned the report would be ready. He promised the report would be ready. Those can report the SAME sentence, and they hand your manager two different men.

Admitted says he conceded something against himself. Complained says he was unhappy rather than useful. Explained says he was being clear.

He is not in the room, and he will be held to the version you gave. That is a real responsibility and it costs you nothing to get right.

So pick the flattest verb that is still accurate. If you are not sure he committed, do not write that he promised.

Nothing in the step back is new

The idea
The backshift reaches only for forms you already own. Present goes to past, present perfect and past go to past perfect, will to would, can to could.

Say needs nobody, tell needs somebody

The idea
Tell takes the listener straight after it, with no to. Say takes no listener at all.

A default, not a law

The idea
When the thing is still true, English often leaves the tense forward. Both versions are correct, and the shifted one is always safe.
still true
She said she is Brazilian.
over and done
She said she was swamped last week.
Both of these are correct English.

The verb is a claim about them

The idea
The reporting verb characterizes somebody who is not in the room. They will be held to your version of them.
a commitment
He promised the report would be ready.
small talk
He mentioned the report would be ready.
Same words. Two different men.

Watch out

Tell takes a person. Say does not. Say “she told me,” never “she said me,” and never “she told to me.” The listener goes straight after tell, with nothing in between.

Nothing takes to after the step back. Will becomes would and it behaves like every other modal you have met. Say “he said he would call,” never “he said he would to call.”

The moves

The frame stays the same. You change only the blue words. The column decides whether the listener is in the sentence.

The listener is not named

  • She said she was [swamped].
  • He said he had [finished].
  • They said they would [come].

The listener is named

  • She told me she was [working on it].
  • He told the client he could [help].
  • She told us she had [sent the files].

Four steps back, and every one of them lands on a form you have already used in this book. The reporting verb is what moves them.

Why this works

You spend a large part of your working day carrying other people's words to somebody who was not there. English marks that with one small move. The reporting verb goes into the past, and everything after it follows one notch. That is the whole machine, and it uses nothing you have not already learned. The harder part is not the tenses. The person you are quoting is absent, and your version of him is the only one in the room.

G

Over to you

No checking here. This one is yours.

In pairs. Have a two minute conversation about your week. Then report it to your partner as if he were a third person, five sentences at least. He listens for one thing only: any place where your reporting verb made him sound different from how he sounded.

On your own. Write three things people actually told you this week. Then go back and ask, for each one, whether a heavier verb would have been unfair and whether a flatter one would have lost something true.
