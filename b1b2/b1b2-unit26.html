ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn to speak from a point in the future. By the end, you can say what will be under way when you get there, and what will already be behind you.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Stand at the future moment. Is it still happening, or already behind you?

Unit 25 asked this about a moment in the past. The question is the same one aimed forwards, so find the future moment first and then ask it.

THE FORM

done before that momentwill have + past participleI'll have finished
how long, up to that momentwill have been + -ingI'll have been working
negative and questionwon't have / will + personWill you be joining us?

The same move, aimed forwards

Unit 25 put you at a moment in the past and asked what was true when it arrived. This unit does exactly that, one direction over.

Pick the future moment. Thursday afternoon. Friday morning. This time next year. Then stand there and look around.

One word stays the same in all three forms, and it is will. What changes is what comes after it, and that is the only thing you are choosing.

Be and -ing for in progress. Have and a participle for done. Have been and -ing for how long.

In progress when you get there

“I'll be presenting to the client then.”

This puts Sol inside the action at that hour. Not before it, not after it. In the middle.

That is why it answers a scheduling question so cleanly. Ada does not need the word busy, because the form already stands Sol in front of a client at two o'clock.

It travels with words that name a moment rather than a deadline: at nine, this time tomorrow, then.

Already behind you when you get there

“By Friday I'll have finished the deck.”

Here Sol stands at Friday and looks BACK. The deck is not finished now. It will be finished from the point of view of a day that has not happened.

This is the form for targets, deadlines and milestones.

It wants by in front of the moment, which you met at unit 8.

Add been and an -ing and you get the length instead of the result. “By next month I'll have been working on it for a year.”

That last one reports how long, and it refuses to say when the work ends. Same refusal the continuous made in unit 25.

Asking about a plan, not asking for a decision

This is where the choice becomes yours, and it is the reason this form turns up in polite English constantly.

“Will you join us?” asks the other person to decide, now, in front of you.

“Will you be joining us?” asks what is already in his plan. It hands him a fact to report rather than a commitment to make.

Nothing about it is softer in the words. It is softer because it assumes the answer already exists, so saying no costs him nothing.

Use it when you want an answer and do not want to apply pressure to get one.

Asking without asking for a decision

The idea
This part is your choice. The continuous asks what is already in somebody's plan. The plain future asks him to decide in front of you.
asking him to commit
Will you join us?
asking what he already plans
Will you be joining us?
why the second one is softer
it assumes the answer exists, so saying no costs him nothing
Same question. Only one of them puts him on the spot.

Watch out

After have, the participle. After be, the -ing. Say “we'll have left,” never “we will have leave.” And say “I'll be finishing dinner,” never “I'll be finish dinner.”

Will finish and will have finished are both correct English. “By Friday I will finish it” breaks no rule. It just does not put you at Friday looking back. The perfect does, and that is why targets take it.

The deadline word is by, and you met it at unit 8. By Friday names the last moment you have. Until Friday names the whole wait. Both are English, and only one of them is a deadline.

The moves

The frame stays the same. You change only the blue words. The column you pick decides whether you are standing inside the action or looking back at it.

In progress at that moment

  • This time tomorrow, I'll be [flying].
  • At nine, I'll be [presenting].
  • Don't call at eight; I'll be [putting] the kids to bed.

Done before that moment

  • By Friday, I'll have [finished].
  • By then, we'll have [launched].
  • By June, I'll have been [here] for a year.

The moment falls inside the first bar and after the second one.

Why this works

These two forms let you speak from a day that has not arrived, as if you were already standing on it. That is most of how work gets planned. You tell somebody not to call at eight because you will be putting the children to bed. The sentence books the hour without an argument. You tell a client that by December you will have shipped, and that sets a target he can hold you to. Pick the day first. Then say whether you will be inside the work or looking back at it.
