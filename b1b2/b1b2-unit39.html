Where it is, where it is going, and what it is for.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn one three-way distinction and use it for two things. By the end, you can place an event in time and a person in a building with the same three words.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Is this where something IS, where it is GOING, or what it is FOR?

Three answers, and each one hands you a group. WHERE IT IS takes at, on or in, and then a second question sorts those three. WHERE IT IS GOING takes to. WHAT IT IS FOR takes for.

Three words, and one question

English uses the same three prepositions for when something happens and for where something is. That is not a coincidence and it is not two lists.

At is a POINT. A point on a clock, or a point on a map. At nine. At the corner.

On is a SURFACE or a line. A day is a square on a calendar. A table has a top. On Monday. On the table.

In is an INSIDE. A month is a container you are somewhere within. So is a room. In June. In the room.

So there is one question to ask, and it is the same question both times: point, surface, or inside?

Time, from the widest to the narrowest

Watch the three words narrow as the period gets smaller.

In holds the big containers. In 2026. In June. In the summer. In the morning.

On takes days and dates, because a day is a square you point at. On Monday. On the fourteenth. On Monday morning.

Note that last one. In the morning becomes on Monday morning, because naming the day makes the whole thing a day.

At takes clock times and a few fixed points. At nine. At noon. At night. At night is the odd one, and it is worth simply learning.

Place, and the same three words again

At is a point on a map, with no interest in what is inside it. At the office. At the corner. At the airport.

In puts you inside something with edges. In the office. In reception. In Lisbon.

The pair is worth holding together. At the office is where you are; in the office is that you are indoors. Kofi uses both in one line.

On is CONTACT WITH A SURFACE, and this is the part that gets stored wrong. It is not on top of.

The surface can lie flat: on the table, on the floor, on the third floor.

It can stand upright: on the wall, on the door, on the screen, on page four.

It can even be above you: on the ceiling. Nothing is resting on anything there. The thing is simply touching a surface.

Lines work the same way, because a line is a thin surface.

On the coast. On the river. On the road to Lisbon.

Cities and countries take in, always. In Lisbon. In Brazil. That one never varies, and it is a common slip.

Going there, and doing it for somebody

At, on and in all answer WHERE SOMETHING IS. Two more words answer the other two questions.

To is movement, or a destination. She flew to Lisbon. Go to the office. Back to work.

It is also the word for sending something to a person. Send it to Ana. Write to me. Explain it to them.

For is purpose, or the person who benefits. We booked the room for a dry run. A room for two.

Here is the pair that costs this reader most, because Portuguese uses one word for both. I sent the report TO Ana means she received it. I wrote the report FOR Ana means I did her job.

Two very different sentences, one preposition apart, and neither one is ungrammatical. Choose by asking whether the person RECEIVED it or BENEFITED from it.

Place before time

One more rule, and it costs nothing to learn.

Inside a sentence, the place goes before the time. I land at the airport in the morning.

The other order is not exactly wrong. It reads as translated, and it is one of the commonest tells in otherwise good English.

You can move the time to the FRONT of the sentence if you want to stress it. In the morning, I land at the airport. That is standard and it is not the same as burying it in the middle.

So there are two good positions for a time phrase, and one poor one, which is between the verb and the place.

How precise you are agreeing to be

This is where the choice becomes yours, and the three words are a scale of commitment.

I will be there in June commits you to a month. On the fourteenth commits you to a day. At nine commits you to an hour.

People hear that scale even when they do not notice it. Answering in June to a question about a date is a way of not answering it.

That is sometimes exactly right. If you genuinely do not know the day, in June is honest and on the fourteenth is a promise you will break.

Kofi does both in one line, and the order matters. He gives the month, then narrows. Widening afterwards would have sounded like a retreat.

Watch out

Days take on, months take in. Say “on Monday” and “in June,” never “in Monday” or “at June.” A day is a square you point at; a month is a container you are inside.

Cities and countries always take in. Say “she lives in London,” never “she lives at London.” A city has edges and you are inside them, however large it is.

Arrive takes at or in, and never to. Say “we arrived at the office” or “we arrived in Lisbon,” never “we arrived to the office.” The to belongs to go, not to arrive.

To delivers it. For does it on somebody's behalf. “I sent the report to Ana” and “I wrote the report for Ana” are both correct and they are different facts. Portuguese uses para for both, so this is the pair to slow down for. Ask whether the person RECEIVED it or BENEFITED from it.

On is contact with a surface, not the top of one. Say “the chart is on the wall,” never “the chart is in the wall” unless it is buried inside it. A wall, a screen and a page are all surfaces, and none of them is horizontal.

AMERICAN AND BRITISH ENGLISH

The two Englishes disagree about one very common phrase, and it is a weekend.

British: at the weekend · at Christmas · in hospital · at university.

American: on the weekend · over Christmas · in the hospital · in college.

This book uses American English, so the exercises take on the weekend. Both are understood everywhere and neither will ever be misunderstood.

The one worth noticing is in hospital against in the hospital. British English drops the article when it means the patient is being treated; American English keeps it. She is in hospital is about her health. She is in the hospital is about where she is standing.

The moves

The frame stays the same. You change only the blue words. The column you pick decides how narrow you are being.

Wide, then narrower

  • I will be there [in] June.
  • I will be there [on] the fourteenth.
  • I will be there [at] nine.

The same three, for place

  • We are meeting [at] the client's office.
  • Wait for me [in] reception.
  • They are [on] the third floor.

Read it down for the idea and across for the proof: the same word, both jobs.

Why this works

These three words are the single largest source of small mistakes for a Portuguese speaker, and small is the problem. Nobody misunderstands in Monday. Everybody notices it. They appear several times in every paragraph you write. One wrong habit repeats itself more often than any grammar error you could make. The good news is that one idea explains all eight pages a reference grammar gives this. Point, surface, inside. Learn the question and you have most of it.
