ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn to choose which piece of a sentence lands. By the end, you can put one word in the light, and know what that implies.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Which piece of this sentence do I want read first?

English has a neutral order and several ways of leaving it. Every one of them costs extra words, and every one of them implies somebody disagrees.

The empty subjects, and why they are here

Two English words can start a sentence while meaning nothing at all: there and it.

There announces that something exists. “There is a problem with the invoice.” It is not a place; it is a placeholder.

It holds the subject slot when the real subject is coming later, or is not a thing at all. “It is late.” “It is difficult to say.”

Learn the pair together, because they compete. There is a problem introduces the problem. It is a problem judges something already introduced.

This unit starts here for one reason. The emptier of the two, it, is what every cleft in the next section is built out of.

Pointing at one piece

The neutral sentence is Mira signed it. No word is more prominent than another.

Now split it. It was Mira who signed it. The empty it holds the front, and the piece you care about goes into the gap after was.

Everything else drops behind who or that, out of the way. The reader has nowhere to look but at the name.

You can point at almost any piece this way. It was the invoice that went missing. It was on Friday that she signed it.

Who for people, that for anything, and that is safe everywhere if you are unsure.

Pointing at the whole answer

The other cleft starts with what and holds the answer back to the end.

“What we need is the original quote.” Compare it with we need the original quote, which says the same thing less forcefully.

The shape builds a small delay. By the time the answer arrives the reader has been waiting for it, and the wait is what makes it emphatic.

The verb after what is singular, because the whole first half counts as one thing. What we need is two more people.

It is the natural shape for a recommendation, which is why it turns up at the end of reports.

Front a negative, and the sentence inverts

Move a negative word to the front and English demands something in return: the subject and the auxiliary swap places.

“I have never seen a file vanish like this” becomes “Never have I seen a file vanish like this.”

The same happens after rarely, seldom, at no point, under no circumstances, and after not only.

“Not only did we warn them, but we put it in writing.”

This is formal and it is loud. One of these in a report is a strong move; two in a paragraph reads as theater.

Even, for the one you would not expect

Even does not change the grammar of anything. It marks one member of a set as the surprising one.

“Even the archive copy is gone.” Of all the places, that was the one that should have held.

It goes directly in front of the piece it is marking, and moving it changes the meaning. Even Nils looked and Nils even looked say different things.

With a negative it becomes not even. “Not even the archive had it.”

It is the cheapest device in this unit. One word, no rearranging, and it still puts something in the light.

Emphasis implies that somebody disagrees

This is where the choice becomes yours, and it is the part that goes wrong in writing.

Every device here costs extra words, and a reader assumes you spent them for a reason. The reason he assumes is CONTRAST.

It was Mira who signed it tells him somebody thought otherwise. If nobody did, you have just started an argument alone.

The same goes for the inversion. Never have I seen in a routine email reads as performance, because the form is doing more work than the content.

So the test is short. Before you emphasize, ask who you are contradicting. If the answer is nobody, write the plain sentence.

Watch out

After a fronted negative, the sentence inverts. It is not optional. Say “never have I seen it,” never “never I have seen it.” The same holds after not only: “not only we did warn them” is not English.

The verb after what is singular. Say “what we need is two more people,” never “what we need are two more people.” The whole first half counts as one thing.

The cleft still needs its who or that. Say “it was Mira who signed it,” never “it was Mira who sign it.” The verb behind it keeps its ordinary form.

The moves

The frame stays the same. You change only the blue words. The column you pick decides which piece of the sentence the reader reads first.

Point at one piece

  • It was [Mira] who signed it.
  • It was the [invoice] that went missing.
  • [Even] the archive copy is gone.

Hold it back, or raise the voice

  • What we need is the [original quote].
  • [Never] have I seen a file vanish like this.
  • Not only did we [warn] them, but we wrote it down.

Same facts, four extra words, and one chip standing above the line.

Why this works

Written English has a neutral order, and leaving it is how you argue. Put one piece in the light and the reader knows which part you expect to be challenged. That is useful when something really is in dispute, and it is expensive when nothing is. Every device here costs words. A reader who cannot find the disagreement you were answering concludes that you are defending yourself against nobody.
