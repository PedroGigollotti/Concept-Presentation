4 · Phrasal or Latinate: the register choice

The same message to a colleague and a client: two verbs, one meaning.

ACCESS

The last piece of machinery, and the idea the whole back of the book is built on. Almost every phrasal verb has a formal, Latinate twin. Neither is better; they fit different rooms. One phrase, factor in, you may not have met. Predict it.

A writer adapting the same update for two audiences: the internal team, and the client.

Lea To the team I'd write “we had to put off the launch and cut back on scope.”

Marcus And to the client?

Lea “We've postponed the launch and reduced the scope.” Same facts, more formal.

Marcus Right. The phrasal version isn't wrong for the client, it's just warmer. Read the room.

Lea And we should factor in their timezone before we send it.

Marcus Good, take their timezone into account. To the team, factor in is perfect.

ANALYSIS

Almost every phrasal verb has a Latinate twin, a single, often longer word borrowed from Latin or French that means roughly the same thing. Put off and postpone. Cut back and reduce. Find out and discover. This pairing is the deepest fact about professional English vocabulary.

Neither twin is better. The phrasal verb is shorter, warmer, and more direct, the language of speech and of colleagues. The Latinate verb is longer, cooler, and more formal, the language of contracts and of distance. Fluency is not knowing one or the other. It's holding both and choosing by the room.

Two columns, one meaning each. The left is warm and direct; the right is formal and cool. Fluency is owning both.

Watch out. Avoiding phrasal verbs is not more professional. Native professionals use phrasal verbs constantly, even in formal settings: carry out, set up, point out. The goal is range, not avoidance.
American / British English. Both varieties overuse heavy Latinate verbs in business writing: utilize for use, leverage for use. This book prefers plain over pompous, and the phrasal verb when it's the natural choice.

A learner who avoids phrasal verbs loses one entire register, the warm, direct, spoken half of professional English, and sounds formal and distant in every room, including the ones that want warmth. Owning both twins is owning the full range.

Meet a Stranger

These three are not taught in this book. Reason from the particle, and note the Latinate twin, then reveal the key.

1. “We hammered out the last differences before signing.”

2. “Let's flesh out the proposal before the pitch.”

3. “She mapped out the whole quarter in an afternoon.”

hammer out: to work through disagreement to reach a deal (twin: negotiate). Out: beaten into final shape.

flesh out: to add detail to something (twin: elaborate). Out: filled and expanded.

map out: to plan in detail (twin: plan). Out: laid fully out.
