3 · Narrative past and past perfect

Running a retro.

ACCESS

Goal. Read the retro and notice the moves in bold. You are spotting the pattern here, not producing it yet.

Jordan (plant manager) Walk me through what happened.

Ana (line supervisor) The line stopped at 2 a.m. We'd already swapped the faulty sensor the night before, so the failure wasn't a surprise.

Jordan Had quality signed off before that?

Ana No. They hadn't finished the inspection when we swapped it, which is really the root cause.

Jordan So the swap jumped ahead of the process.

Ana Exactly. Once we reran the calibration, everything stabilized.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how the simple past carries the main story line while the past perfect marks what happened earlier, and how ordering events this way makes a retro easy to follow.

The structure

A retro is a story with more than one layer of past time, and English needs a way to show which layer came first. The simple past carries the main line of events, told in the order you want the listener to follow. The past perfect steps back to mark something that had already happened before that line begins, so the listener never has to guess which event came first.

Simple past (the main line)

Form
verb + -ed, or the irregular past form
Meaning
the events of the story, told in the order they are reported
Use it to
carry the narrative forward, one event after another
The build failed at 2 a.m.

Past perfect (the earlier layer)

Form
had + past participle
Meaning
an event that had already finished before the main story line begins
Use it to
step back and mark what happened first, without breaking the order of telling
We’d already merged the risky branch the night before.

Watch out

Don't use the past perfect for the main line. If every clause is in the past perfect, there is no main line left to step back from. Keep the story in the simple past and reserve "had" for the one earlier event.

Don't leave the order ambiguous when it matters. "The line stopped. We swapped the sensor." leaves the order unclear. If the swap came first and caused the failure, say so: "We'd swapped the sensor. The line stopped."

American / British English.

Past participle of get. American "we'd gotten the sign-off"; British "we'd got the sign-off." This book uses "gotten."

The moves

Same two-layer structure in every line. The highlighted slot changes; the past perfect always marks the earlier event.

  • We'd already [swapped the sensor / replaced the part] before quality weighed in.
  • By the time we noticed, [the line / the batch] had already failed.
  • They hadn't [finished the inspection / reviewed the change] when we swapped it.
  • Once we'd [recalibrated it / reset the line], things stabilized.
  • Nobody had [flagged the risk / run the check] beforehand.

Where each move sits

The events are the same. The past perfect tells the listener which one came first.

Why this works

A retro only works if the room can reconstruct the sequence without asking follow-up questions, and holding a full timeline in your head while you talk is expensive. The past perfect offloads that cost onto the grammar: it marks "this already happened" in a single word, so you can keep the main line moving in simple past and only reach for "had" when you need to step back. Used well, it turns a pile of facts into a story with a cause: something had already gone wrong before the failure everyone noticed.
