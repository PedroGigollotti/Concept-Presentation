10 · Cleft sentences

Steering emphasis across a paragraph.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn the it-cleft, the wh-cleft, and the all-cleft, and how each steers the reader's focus. By the end you should be able to cleft a sentence to place the emphasis exactly.

The structure

A cleft sentence splits one sentence into two parts so you can shine a light on a single element. English gives you three main shapes. The “it” cleft, “It was the pricing that lost us the deal,” puts one element in the spotlight and pushes everything else into the background, which is perfect for correcting a wrong idea (“It was the pricing, not the product...”). The “what” cleft, “What we need is a clearer brief,” starts with a “what” clause and delays the important part until after “is,” so the reader reaches your point already prepared for it. The “all” cleft, “All we changed was the headline,” narrows the focus to one thing and quietly makes it sound small. In every case you are choosing where the reader's attention lands, and steering it on purpose.

The it-cleft

Form
It + be + focused element + that / who + the rest
Meaning
splits the sentence to spotlight one element and background everything else
Use it to
single out the one thing that matters, or correct a wrong assumption
It was the pricing, not the product, that lost us the deal.

The “what” cleft

Form
What + clause + be + focused element
Meaning
fronts a “what” clause and puts the new or important part after “be”
Use it to
build up to the point, or frame what you are about to say
What we need is a clearer brief. / What surprised me was the cost.

The all-cleft (and reversed wh-cleft)

Form
All (that) + clause + be + X; or X + be + what / all ...
Meaning
narrows focus to a single thing, often to minimize; or reverses to front the focus
Use it to
downplay (“all we did was...”) or place the focus first
All we changed was the headline. / A clearer brief is what we need.

Watch out

Agree the relative verb with the focus. In an it-cleft, “it is / it was” stays singular, but the relative verb agrees with the focused noun: “It is the numbers that show the gap,” not “that shows.”

Don't over-cleft. Clefting every sentence flattens the emphasis; one cleft in a paragraph steers attention, five compete. Spotlight everything and you spotlight nothing.

American / British English.

Who vs that for people. “It was Marco who signed” is standard in both; American English also readily allows “It was Marco that signed.” This book uses “who” for people and “that” for things.

Reduced “what” clefts. Casual American speech drops the frame (“Thing is, we're late”); formal writing in both varieties keeps the full cleft. This book uses the full form.

The moves

Same content, one thing spotlighted. Keep the frame; change only the slot and the situation.

  • It was [the pricing / the timing] that lost us the deal.
  • What we need is [a clearer brief / more time].
  • All we changed was [the headline / one line].
  • It wasn't the product but [the price / the timing] that hurt us.
  • What surprised me was [the cost / the speed].

Where each move sits

Same fact, sharper focus. The cleft splits the sentence to spotlight the one thing that mattered.

Why this works

Here is why clefts are worth the extra words. A plain sentence spreads its emphasis evenly, so the reader has to guess which part you most want them to hold on to. A cleft removes the guesswork: it separates the one element you care about and frames it, so “It was the tone that caused the problem” cannot be misread, where “The tone caused the problem” might slip past. The “what” cleft adds a second gift: by opening with “What we need is,” you make the reader wait for the point, and a point they waited for lands harder than one that simply arrived. This is why a single cleft can steer a whole paragraph. The cost is that the effect is limited: spotlight everything and you spotlight nothing, so cleft the one sentence that carries the paragraph and leave the rest in plain order.

1 · Under pressure

A cleft is how you stop one fact getting lost in a sentence full of other facts. Forty seconds each.

2 · Say it

A cleft costs extra words, so it has to buy something. What it buys is a pause the listener can hear.

Record situation 2. Play it back and listen for one thing: is there a small pause before “honest feedback”? The whole point of the wh-cleft is that the reader waits. If you rushed straight through it, the structure is there and the effect is not.

3 · With a partner, if you have one

debate a small decision (which tool to adopt, where to hold the offsite). Every time you make a key point, force it into a cleft: “What matters here is...,” “It's the cost, not the features, that...,” “All I'm really asking is....” Your partner listens for whether the cleft actually sharpened the point or just padded it, and calls out the ones that added nothing.

Then, take a flat paragraph you wrote recently and rewrite exactly one sentence in it as a cleft, choosing the sentence whose emphasis you most want the reader to catch. Notice that one cleft steers the whole paragraph, where three would cancel each other out.
