30 · Backshift and reporting nuance

Staying faithful to what was actually said.

ACCESS

Incoming deal lead Is that still true today?

Outgoing deal lead I'm not sure, that's just what she said then. She also said the buyer had already signed.

Incoming deal lead Good to know the timing of each claim.

Outgoing deal lead Exactly, I wanted to keep the timing clear for you.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how backshift in reported speech marks when a claim was true, and when to skip it to keep a claim faithfully anchored to the present.

The structure

Reporting what someone said always involves a choice about time: has the situation changed since they said it, or is it still true? The traditional rule shifts the tense back one step, present becomes past, past becomes past perfect, which marks the claim as belonging to that earlier moment. But when the fact reported is still true right now, skipping the backshift and keeping the original tense is often more accurate, and increasingly standard, especially in handoffs where the current status matters.

Backshift

Form
present becomes past; past becomes past perfect
Meaning
marks a reported claim as anchored to the time it was originally said
Use it to
report a claim faithfully without asserting it is still true now
She said the numbers were still holding, as of last week. She also said the buyer had already signed.

No backshift (still true now)

Form
keep the original tense of the reported claim
Meaning
marks a reported claim as still true at the time of reporting
Use it to
report a claim that remains accurate right now, especially in a handoff
She mentioned the deal is still on track for March.

Watch out

Don't backshift a claim you know is still true. "She said the launch was on track" can wrongly imply doubt about whether it still is. If you know it's still true, say "she said the launch is on track."

Don't skip backshift when the timing genuinely matters. If a claim may be outdated, keeping the present tense can mislead the next person. Use the past or past perfect to flag that it was true only as of when it was said.

American / British English.

Flexibility of backshift. Both varieties allow skipping backshift for a still-true fact, but American business writing has grown more comfortable dropping it even in fairly formal handoffs. British formal writing tends to keep the traditional backshift a bit more consistently. This book uses whichever choice best reflects whether the fact is still current.

The moves

Same timing precision in every line. The highlighted slot changes; the tense choice always signals whether the claim still holds.

  • She said the numbers were [holding / improving], as of last week.
  • She mentioned the deal is [still on track / slightly delayed].
  • He said the buyer had already [signed / paid the deposit].
  • They said the terms [hold / were holding] as of this morning.
  • She noted that the risk is [still open / mostly resolved].

Where each move sits

The same report, tensed differently depending on whether the fact still holds.

Why this works

A handoff is only useful if the next person can tell which facts are current and which ones might already be stale, and backshift is the grammar's built-in tool for marking that difference. Skipping backshift is a small but real claim: this is still true, not just true when I heard it. Getting this right protects both you and the next person, because a claim wrongly presented as current can send someone down the wrong path, and a claim needlessly backshifted can make settled information sound more uncertain than it actually is.
