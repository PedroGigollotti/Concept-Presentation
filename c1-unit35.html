35 · Collocation and colligation

The words that travel together.

ACCESS

Goal. Read the review and notice the moves in bold. You are spotting the pattern here, not producing it yet.

Editor This draft reads a bit off.

Writer What do you mean?

Editor You wrote 'make a decision', which is fine, but you also wrote 'do a mistake' and 'make a risk' the wrong way around. It should be 'make a mistake' and 'take a risk.'

Writer Ah, I always mix those up.

Editor Common trap. We also say 'heavy rain' but 'strong coffee,' not the reverse.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how collocations pair specific words together by convention, not logic, and how to catch the common mismatches that mark non-native writing.

The structure

Some word pairings in English aren't governed by grammar or logic at all, just convention, and native speakers absorb them without ever noticing the rule. Make a decision is standard; do a decision is simply wrong, even though do and make are near-synonyms elsewhere. Heavy rain and strong coffee follow the same arbitrary logic: swap the adjectives and the phrase instantly sounds foreign, even though both describe intensity.

Verb-noun collocations

Form
specific verb + specific noun, fixed by convention
Meaning
a pairing that native speakers use automatically, with no logical reason for the choice
Use it to
avoid the most common non-native mismatches in everyday professional English
Make a decision, not do a decision. Take a risk, not do a risk.

Adjective-noun collocations

Form
specific adjective + specific noun, fixed by convention
Meaning
an intensity or quality pairing that cannot be freely swapped for a near-synonym
Use it to
sound natural describing weather, quality, or degree
Heavy rain, not strong rain. Strong coffee, not heavy coffee.

Watch out

Don't assume synonyms share the same collocations. "Do" and "make" overlap in meaning but not in which nouns they pair with. "Do a mistake" is wrong; "make a mistake" is correct.

Don't translate collocations directly from another language. Many languages pair intensity words differently. When in doubt, check the specific pairing rather than assuming logic will carry over.

American / British English.

"Have a shower" versus "take a shower." British English commonly says "have a shower" or "have a break." American English strongly prefers "take a shower" or "take a break." This book uses the American "take" pattern.

The moves

Same fixed pairing in every line. The highlighted slot changes; the verb or adjective is locked to its noun by convention, not logic.

  • We need to make a [decision / final call] by Friday.
  • Let's not take a [chance / risk] on this one.
  • We're seeing [heavy demand / strong growth] this quarter.
  • That was a costly [mistake / error] to make.
  • Let's take [a break / five minutes] before the next session.

Where each move sits

The same meaning, now paired with the verb English speakers actually expect.

Why this works

Collocation errors rarely block understanding, the listener almost always knows what you mean, but they are one of the most reliable tells of a non-native speaker precisely because there's no logical rule to reason your way to the right answer. The only real fix is exposure: noticing which specific words travel together in professional English and storing the pairing as a fixed chunk, the same way a native speaker does, rather than trying to reconstruct it from vocabulary you already know.
