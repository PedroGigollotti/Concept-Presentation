ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn to say a claim you cannot fully back, at exactly the strength you can defend. By the end you can hedge the part that is uncertain and leave the rest alone.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

How much of this can I actually back?

The hedge has to match the evidence you hold. Too little and you claim more than you can support. Too much and the listener no longer knows what you are claiming.

THE FORM

the conclusion is uncertainshould / may + base verbIt should respond well.
the degree is uncertainreasonably / largely / to some extentIt responds reasonably well.
the source is not youthe data suggests / it seems to / it appears toThe data suggests a six-month limit.

Three different things can be uncertain

Look at what Kofi said. “It should respond reasonably well, though the data suggests we'll see limits around the six-month mark.”

Three hedges, and no two of them do the same job. Should softens the CONCLUSION: it may not respond at all. Reasonably limits the DEGREE: it will not respond very well. The data suggests names the SOURCE of the claim as the evidence rather than the speaker.

That is the whole grammar of hedging. Before you choose a word, name which of the three is uncertain. If none of them is, you do not need one.

The test you can run with no book

Say the sentence with every hedge removed, and ask whether you would defend that sentence in the meeting.

If you would, the hedge added nothing and it goes. If you would not, the hedge stays, and it has to name WHICH part you cannot support: the conclusion, the degree, or the source.

Kofi's line without hedges is “It will respond well”. He would not defend that; his own next clause names a six-month limit. So both hedges are doing work, and they are hedging different things.

The test cannot see one case, and it is worth knowing which. A hedge used for POLITENESS survives the removal: “I'd suggest we wait” becomes “We should wait”, which the speaker would defend, and removing the hedge makes him blunt. This test measures how certain you are. Unit 16 measures how direct you want to be.

When hedging costs you authority

Read Kofi's second answer again. “Not necessarily.” Two words, no hedge.

He knows the answer, so hedging it would make him sound evasive. And two hedges on one claim are not twice as careful: it may possibly seem to suggest states almost nothing, and the listener notices immediately.

One hedge per claim, on the part that is actually uncertain. If you find yourself using two, check that they are hedging two different things, as Kofi's are, and not the same thing twice.

What the hedge tells the listener

Dr. Reyes ends with “Fair enough, flag it if that changes.” She has an answer she can plan around, from somebody who told her where it stops being reliable.

That is what a hedge is for, and it is not politeness. An unhedged claim that turns out to be wrong makes your next three claims less believable. A hedged one that turns out to be wrong costs nothing, because you already said how far your certainty went.

So the question behind the question: are you protecting the claim, or protecting yourself? The first is what this unit teaches. The second is what four hedges in one sentence are doing.

Watch out

Do not use two hedges on one claim. Say “It should respond well.” Never “It may possibly seem to respond somewhat well.” Four hedges do not make a claim four times as careful. They make it impossible to read, and the listener stops trying.

Do not hedge what you can support. If you know the answer, give it. “Not necessarily” is a complete answer. A hedge in front of a fact you hold makes you sound evasive, and it is the C1 error that costs the most authority.

The moves

Same claim in every line. The slot changes; the column states how much of it you can support.

What you can back

  • Response rates [are up nine percent / have risen nine percent].
  • The trial [closes / ends] Friday.
  • It [is not / was never] a hard limit.

What you cannot back yet

  • Response [should hold / should stay steady], though the sample is small.
  • The readout [should land / should be ready] Friday.
  • It [seems to depend / appears to depend] on patient history.

A confident claim sits on the left. A carefully qualified claim sits on the right.

Why this works

A claim that turns out to be wrong makes your next three less believable. Hedging is how you go on saying useful things before you can prove them, and stay believable when one of them is wrong. The skill is not knowing the words. It is knowing which part of your own sentence you cannot support.
