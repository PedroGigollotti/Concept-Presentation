15 · Hedging

Making a claim you can defend.

ACCESS

Goal. Read the review and notice the moves in bold. You are spotting the pattern here, not producing it yet.

Dr. Reyes (clinical lead) Will this treatment approach hold up?

Kofi (lab director) It should respond reasonably well, though the data suggests we'll see limits around the six-month mark.

Dr. Reyes Is that a hard limit?

Kofi Not necessarily. It seems to depend heavily on patient response.

Dr. Reyes Fair enough, flag it if that changes.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how hedging language lets you make a claim you can defend, without either overstating certainty or sounding evasive.

The structure

A claim you cannot fully back with evidence still needs to be said, and hedging is the tool that lets you say it responsibly. Modal verbs like should and may, verbs like seem and suggest, and adverbs like reasonably and largely all soften a claim just enough to stay honest about your uncertainty, without undercutting the claim so much that it sounds like you have nothing to say.

Hedging modals and verbs

Form
should / may + base verb; seem to / appear to / suggest + clause
Meaning
a claim softened to reflect genuine uncertainty
Use it to
state a likely conclusion without overcommitting to it
It should respond reasonably well. The data suggests we'll see limits around the six-month mark.

Hedging adverbs

Form
reasonably / largely / to some extent + adjective or clause
Meaning
a claim scoped to a degree, not an absolute
Use it to
limit how far a claim is meant to apply
It seems to depend heavily on patient response.

Watch out

Don't hedge so much that the claim disappears. “It might possibly perhaps scale somewhat” stacks so many hedges that there's no claim left. Pick one hedge and commit to the rest of the sentence.

Don't hedge a fact that doesn't need it. “The test seems to have failed” is odd if you can just check the log. Reserve hedges for genuine uncertainty, not things you could simply verify.

American / British English.

Quite as a hedge. British English uses quite as a mild downtoner (quite good means fairly good). American English often reads quite good as stronger, closer to very good. This book avoids quite for hedging and uses reasonably or fairly instead, to avoid the ambiguity.

All three are correct here. They hedge the extent, not the certainty. The three run at almost the same frequency in American English, but “largely” and “mainly” read as written forms while “mostly” is the one you also hear.

The moves

Same softened claim in every line. The highlighted slot changes; the hedge sets exactly how firm the claim is.

  • It should [respond well / hold up fine] under normal conditions.
  • The data suggests [we'll see limits / response varies].
  • It seems to [depend on / correlate with] patient history.
  • This is largely a [dosage issue / timing issue], as far as we can tell.
  • To some extent, it [depends on / varies with] the patient profile.

Where each move sits

A confident claim sits on the left. A carefully qualified claim sits on the right.

Why this works

A claim that turns out to be wrong costs you credibility, and hedging is how careful speakers protect themselves without refusing to answer. Each hedge word does a small, specific job: should signals a likely outcome, seem to signals an impression rather than a proven fact, and an adverb like largely scopes a claim so it isn't read as absolute. Used well, hedging lets you say something useful under real uncertainty, rather than forcing a choice between a risky overclaim and an unhelpful silence.
