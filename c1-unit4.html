Forecasting the quarter.

Wei (finance) What's the read on Q3?

Sofia (brand director) Revenue will likely dip in July, then recover. We're launching the new campaign on the 1st, which should help.

Wei And the rebrand?

Sofia We will have shipped it by end of quarter. By the time we report back, we'll have closed the gap.

Wei Good. Put a firm date on the rebrand for the board.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how plain future forms forecast and the future perfect commits to a deadline result, and how the choice signals how firm your promise is.

The structure

Forecasting always carries an implicit bet: are you predicting, or promising? Will makes a prediction or an in-the-moment decision, with no particular deadline attached. The future perfect makes a commitment: it names a point in the future and says the action will be complete by then. Choosing between them is choosing how much you are willing to be held to.

Will (plain future)

Form
will + base verb
Meaning
a prediction or an in-the-moment decision, without a fixed plan
Use it to
forecast a trend or make a call without committing to a deadline
Revenue will likely dip in July.

Future perfect

Form
will have + past participle
Meaning
an action viewed as complete by a specific future point
Use it to
commit to a result by a stated deadline
We will have shipped v2 by the end of Q3.

Watch out

Don't use will for a fixed, calendared plan. “We will launch Tuesday” reads like a guess. If it's already on the calendar, say “We're launching Tuesday.”

Don't drop the deadline marker with the future perfect. “We will have finished Friday” is missing its anchor. Say by Friday so the deadline is explicit.

American / British English.

Shall for formal commitments. British formal and legal English keeps shall for first-person commitments: “We shall provide the report by Monday.” American English uses will in nearly all of these contexts. This book uses will.

Dropping on before a weekday. American English routinely leaves the preposition out: We're launching Tuesday, The audit finishes Friday, We start Monday. British English keeps it: We're launching on Tuesday. Both are correct, and you will see the bare form throughout this book. Read launching Tuesday and launching on Tuesday as the same thing.

The moves

Same choice in every line. The highlighted slot changes; will forecasts and the future perfect commits to a deadline.

  • We'll likely see [a dip / a rebound] in Q3.
  • We're launching [the campaign / the new tier] on Tuesday.
  • We will have shipped [the creative / the rebrand] by end of quarter.
  • By the time we report back, we'll have [closed / tested] the gap.
  • We're set to [wrap up / kick off] the campaign next week.

Where each move sits

A prediction sits on the left. A deadline you can be held to sits on the right.

Why this works

Every forecast is also a promise about how much risk you are willing to own, and English lets you set that risk level with one grammar choice instead of a paragraph of hedging. Will keeps the door open: it is a best guess, not a deadline. The future perfect closes the door on a specific date: will have done X by Y is a claim someone can hold you to later. Reach for the plain future when you are reading a trend, and reach for the future perfect only when you actually intend to be checked against that date. Mixing them up either overpromises a guess or undersells a real deadline.
