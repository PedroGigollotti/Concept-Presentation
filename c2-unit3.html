3 · The subjunctive

Formal recommendation and set phrases.

ACCESS

Chair The committee needs a formal recommendation on the leave policy.

Priya We recommend that the policy be revised to include caregiver leave.

Marcus And that it take effect next quarter, not next year.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn the three subjunctive forms, what they mean, and when their formality is worth it. By the end you should be able to form the subjunctive and use it on purpose.

The structure

The subjunctive is a special verb form used after certain words to talk about what should happen, rather than what does happen. Its key feature is simple: you use the base form of the verb, with no ending, even where you would normally add one. After verbs like “recommend,” “insist,” “demand,” and “require,” you say “we recommend that he be present” and “she insisted that the report be revised,” not “is” or “was.” It also appears in a set of fixed phrases: “be that as it may,” “if need be,” “come what may.” The subjunctive sounds formal and is common in official and legal English, so it is worth controlling even though everyday speech often avoids it.

The subjunctive

Form
verb or adjective of recommending / requiring (recommend, insist, demand, ask, essential that, vital that) + that + subject + base verb
Meaning
presents an action as required or advised, not as a fact
Use it to
make a formal recommendation, demand, or requirement
The auditor insists that the report be signed by Friday. (not “is signed”)

The formulaic subjunctive

Form
fixed expressions that preserve the old subjunctive: be that as it may, suffice it to say, come what may, so be it, far be it from me
Meaning
set idioms in which the subjunctive survives frozen
Use it to
add a formal, slightly literary note to a concession or an aside
Be that as it may, the deadline stands.

The “were” subjunctive

Form
were (in every person) after if, wish, as if, as it were, suppose, would rather
Meaning
marks something as hypothetical or contrary to fact
Use it to
signal unreality clearly in formal speech and writing
She speaks as if she were already in charge. / I wish it were simpler.

Watch out

No -s, no past, no “to.” The subjunctive is the bare base form: “that he be,” “that she attend,” “that it not be delayed,” never “that he is,” “to be,” or “was.”

Negate with “not” plus the base verb. Write “that he not attend,” not “that he doesn't attend” or “that he not attends.” No “do” and no ending.

American / British English.

Subjunctive vs “should.” American English prefers the bare subjunctive (“recommend that he be present”); British English often adds “should” (“recommend that he should be present”). This book uses the bare subjunctive.

How alive it is. The subjunctive is common in American formal writing; in British English the “should” version is a frequent alternative. Both are correct; this book teaches the American form.

The moves

Same requirement, formal mood. Keep the frame; change only the slot and the situation.

  • We recommend that [the policy / the fee] be revised.
  • It is essential that [each change / every step] be documented.
  • The board insists that [the report / the vote] take place today.
  • We ask that [he / she] not be excluded.
  • Be that as it may, the deadline stands.

Where each move sits

The same demand, escalating in formality. The bare base form is the formal register's default.

Why this works

Here is why this small form matters. When you say “I recommend that he submit the report,” the plain base verb “submit” quietly marks the sentence as formal and correct, and a careful reader notices at once. The common mistake is to change it to “submits,” which sounds natural to many learners but reads as an error in formal writing. The safest approach is to learn the trigger words as a fixed list, “recommend, suggest, insist, demand, require, request, propose, essential that, important that,” and to remember that they all take the base verb after “that.” The fixed phrases are even easier: you just memorize them whole. Get this right and your formal writing gains a small but real mark of polish; get it wrong and one “submits” can weaken an otherwise strong document.

1 · Under pressure

The subjunctive is a fixed form after a fixed trigger, so the only real work is deciding whether the moment deserves it. Forty-five seconds each.

2 · Say it

The bare base form is almost inaudible, which is exactly why learners drop it. Hearing yourself say it is how it becomes automatic.

Record situation 1 and situation 2 back to back. Play them back and listen for one thing: did you say be and complete, or did an -s or an is creep in? If it did, say each line five more times until the bare form stops feeling like an error.

3 · With a partner, if you have one

hold a two-minute board meeting. One of you proposes a policy change using the subjunctive (“I move that the policy be revised,” “It is essential that the change take effect at once”); the other responds formally, agreeing or objecting, using at least one formulaic subjunctive (“Be that as it may,” “Suffice it to say”). Swap roles and run a second motion.

Then, take a routine request you would normally send casually and write it twice, once in everyday English and once with the subjunctive. Read both aloud and decide which the situation actually calls for. The goal is control of register, not constant formality.
