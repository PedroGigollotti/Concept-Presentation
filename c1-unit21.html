Focusing on the one thing that matters.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how the it-cleft singles out exactly one element for focus, and how to use it to correct a misattribution cleanly.

The structure

When several things could plausibly explain an outcome, a plain sentence leaves the listener to guess which one you mean is the real cause. The it-cleft removes that ambiguity by building the whole sentence around a single focused element: It was X that... names X as the one thing that matters, and everything else in the clause is background. It is especially useful for correcting a wrong assumption, because it can name the wrong candidate and the right one in the same breath.

It-cleft (basic focus)

Form
It + is or was + focused element + that or who + rest of clause
Meaning
singles out one element as the important or contrastive one
Use it to
focus attention on exactly one piece of information
It was the pricing change that drove the increase.

Negative it-cleft (correction)

Form
It wasn’t X that ..., it was Y that ...
Meaning
explicitly rules out one candidate and points to the correct one
Use it to
correct a misattribution in a single, clean move
It wasn’t the marketing that drove it, it was the pricing change.

Watch out

Don't use "who" for a non-person. "It was the pricing who drove it" is wrong. Use "that" for things: "It was the pricing that drove it."

Don't overuse it-clefts for information that needs no contrast. A plain sentence is often clearer. Reserve the it-cleft for genuine focus or correction, not routine statements.

American / British English.

Contracting "it's" in formal writing. British formal writing more readily allows "it's the pricing that..." in reports. American formal writing tends to spell out "it is" in the same register. This book spells out "it is" in formal contexts.

The moves

Same focus in every line. The highlighted slot changes; the frame always singles out one element.

  • It was the [pricing change / new feature] that drove the increase.
  • It wasn't [marketing / timing] that caused it, it was the pricing.
  • It's the [data / feedback], not the opinions, that should decide this.
  • It was [Priya / the support team] who caught the issue first.
  • It is [retention / acquisition] that matters most this quarter.

Where each move sits

The same fact, now framed so there is no doubt about which cause matters.

Why this works

When a room is debating which of several plausible causes is the real one, a flat sentence doesn't settle the debate, it just adds one more opinion to the pile. The it-cleft settles it structurally: by building the sentence around a single named cause, it leaves no room to misread which factor you mean. That is exactly why it is so useful for correcting a wrong assumption: naming the wrong cause and the right one inside the same frame does the correcting work in one clean, memorable sentence.
