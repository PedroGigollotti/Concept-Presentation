ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn to summarize sources faithfully, compare them without blurring, and evaluate before you recommend. By the end you should be able to turn two conflicting sources into one supported recommendation.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Am I giving the claim, or the topic?

A topic is what a source is about. A claim is what it asserts. Only the second can be compared, tested or disagreed with.

The structure

The last C2 skill brings the others together: taking two or more sources and producing one fair, evaluated recommendation. It has three steps, and they must stay separate. First, summarize each source fairly, catching its actual claim, not just its topic, so “the report argues that remote work trades team feeling for output,” not “the report is about remote work.” Second, attribute and compare: keep each source's view distinct and show where they agree and where they differ, “both agree costs fell, but they disagree about the risk.” Third, judge and combine: weigh the sources by things like sample size, how recent they are, and how independent they are, and only then draw a careful conclusion, “the second report uses broader, more recent data, so on balance its warning is the safer bet.” The whole skill uses the reporting verbs, linking words, hedges, and comparatives from earlier units, working together.

Faithful summary

Form
capture the main claim plus key support in your own words; drop detail, keep proportion
Meaning
a shorter version that preserves the source's actual position
Use it to
give a reader the gist without misrepresenting it
The report argues that remote work raised output but weakened cohesion. (claim, balanced)

Attribution and comparison

Form
reporting verbs + sources + contrast markers: A finds X, whereas B finds Y; both agree that ..., but diverge on ...
Meaning
keeps each source's view distinct and shows where they meet and part
Use it to
compare two accounts without blurring them together
The first report emphasizes cost; the second, by contrast, stresses risk.

Evaluation and synthesis

Form
evaluative language + hedged conclusion: more robust, based on a larger sample, though limited by ...; on balance ...
Meaning
judge the sources' quality, then draw a supported conclusion
Use it to
turn a comparison into a recommendation
B's data is more recent and broader, so on balance its conclusion is the safer bet.

A topic is not a summary

The report is about remote work tells a reader nothing they can use. The report argues that remote work lowers retention gives them something to accept, doubt or test.

The verb is where the difference lives: argues, claims, finds, warns, concludes, concedes. Each names the kind of move the source is making.

Finds reports evidence, argues reports reasoning, warns reports a risk being raised. Choosing accurately is already half the evaluation, before you have said a word of your own.

The test you can run with no book

Ask: could someone disagree with what I just said about the source?

If not, you gave a topic. The report is about cost cannot be disagreed with; the report argues the cost case is decisive can.

The mechanical half keeps your voice out of theirs: the reporting verb is the source's move, and your verdict needs its own clause. The report argues X; the argument rests on a single quarter of data. Two clauses, two owners.

Positioning two sources against each other

By contrast, similarly, whereas, where the first sees ... the second sees: these say how the sources stand to one another without saying which is right.

That separation matters in a research note, because a reader has to be able to see the disagreement before they see your recommendation.

Arun's by contrast does exactly that, and nobody in the room yet knows what he thinks.

Common ground is the useful half

Both accept that the upside is real. Two reports with opposite conclusions still agree somewhere, and naming that shrinks the decision.

It is also the fastest test of whether you have read both properly: a summary that finds no common ground has usually summarised the conclusions and not the arguments.

So the shape of a good source summary is three moves: what each claims, how they stand to each other, and where they agree. The verdict comes fourth, in a clause of its own.

Watch out

Summarize the argument, not the topic. “The report is about remote work” tells the reader nothing; “the report argues remote work trades cohesion for output” tells them the claim. A summary must carry the position, not just the subject.

Separate summary from evaluation. First say faithfully what each source claims; then, distinctly, say what you think of it. Blending the two (the report wrongly claims...) hides the source's actual position behind your verdict.

American / British English.

While in comparison. British whilst A argues...; American while A argues... This book uses while and whereas.

Quotation punctuation. American English puts commas and periods inside quotation marks (cohesion, she wrote); British places them outside. This book puts them inside.

The moves

Same sources, one verdict. Keep the frame; change only the slot and the situation.

  • The report argues that [remote work / the policy] raised output.
  • Both sources agree that [costs / risks] matter.
  • A stresses cost; B, by contrast, stresses risk.
  • B's data is [more recent / broader], so it's more robust.
  • On balance, the second case is stronger.

Where each move sits

Two accounts, one judgment. Synthesis attributes each source, weighs them, and lands a supported conclusion.

Why this works

Here is why this is the hardest skill and the right one to end on. A recommendation built on sources is only as trustworthy as the fairness of its summaries, and the strong temptation is to bend each source toward the answer you already prefer, softening the one you like and sharpening the one you don't, until the evaluation is really just your own opinion wearing citations. A reader who knows the sources will notice; a reader who doesn't will be misled. So the honesty of the whole thing depends on summarizing each source the way its own author would recognize, before you say a word about which is better. That is why you keep the two steps apart: here is what A says, here is what B says, here is where they agree and differ, and only then, here is why one is stronger and what I recommend. Done this way, your judgment earns its authority from the fair summary that came first.

3 · With a partner, if you have one

each of you reads out two short, conflicting findings on the same question (invent them: “Source A says the campaign worked; Source B says it didn't”). Your partner must, in one spoken turn, summarize both faithfully, name where they agree and differ, evaluate which is more credible and why, and land a recommendation, using markers like whereas, more robust, and on balance. Then swap. The test: did the summary stay fair before the verdict arrived?

Then, find two short sources that disagree (two reviews, two articles, two colleagues' emails) and write a single paragraph that summarizes each, compares them, weighs their reliability, and ends with your recommendation. Check that a reader could tell what each source actually said before they could tell what you concluded.
