Why this mattersWhere listening actually breaks down

Some word pairs do not just link. They fuse into a new sound that belongs to neither word.

Would you becomes wouldja. Don't you becomes doncha. Got you becomes gotcha. What are you becomes wadaya.

This is where most listening comprehension actually breaks down. These are extremely common phrases, and once fused they are unrecognizable if you are listening for the written forms.

This is where most listening actually breaks down.
Producing these is optional. Recognizing them is not. You can be perfectly understood without ever fusing anything. But an American colleague saying Wouldja send me that? is producing a phrase you know perfectly well in a form you have never heard.

How to make itTwo sounds meeting in the middle

The main rule: a t or a d before a y sound fuses with it. T plus y becomes ch, so don't you becomes doncha and got you becomes gotcha. D plus y becomes j, so would you becomes wouldja and did you becomes didja.

The picture: two sounds meeting in the middle and agreeing on a third.

A second rule: sounds change to match their neighbors. Ten bikes becomes tem bikes, because the n moves to the lips to match the b. Good boy becomes goob boy. Next day becomes nex day, dropping a consonant that is hard to say between two others.

The way in, and this is the productive task. Play a sentence and write down what you hear, not what is spelled. Writing what you hear forces you to abandon the spelling, which is the actual barrier.

Then test the real goal. Play the sentences without reading them and check you can follow. That is what this unit is for.

Why does this unit say you do not have to learn to say wouldja?

Because saying it clearly costs you nothing. The problem is hearing it. An American will say wouldja whether or not you do, and you need to recognize it.
