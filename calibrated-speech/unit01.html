Why this mattersYou already have most of them

English has about 24 consonants, and here is the good news: you already have most of them. If you spend equal time on all 24 you will waste most of your practice.

So this first unit is not practice. It is a map. By the end you will know which small handful of consonants actually need your attention, and you can stop worrying about the rest.

For almost everyone there are four groups that need real work: th, the American r, the l at the end of a word, and the h.

This first unit is not practice. It is a map.
Think of it like moving to a new city. Most of the streets are already familiar. You only need a map for a few.

What your language doesFind out which ones are yours

The th sounds are missing from most of the world's languages. Assume they are new for you unless you already know otherwise.

The American r is a specific tongue movement that very few languages use, even languages that have some kind of r. Two separate things can be wrong: the sound of your r, and whether it survives after a vowel in car and hard.

The l at the end of a word goes wrong in two opposite directions. Some languages turn it into a w, so milk becomes miwk. Others keep it light and clear, which sounds too thin for American English.

The h is silent in the spelling of several European languages and absent altogether in others. If your language writes h without saying it, you will probably drop it in house and then over-correct and add one in away.

How to make itSay each group and mark it

Nothing to build yet. This unit is diagnosis.

Say each group out loud, then decide: easy, or hard. Trust your own judgment, because it is usually right. A sound that feels the same as another sound is exactly the sound that will need the most listening work later.
