Why this mattersA vowel that changes while you say it

Some English vowels are not one sound. They start in one place and glide to another while you are still saying them: the o in no, the a in day, the i in time.

If your language has pure vowels that hold still, you will produce these as single steady sounds, and the result is a clear accent marker on very common words. No becomes a flat noh. Day becomes deh.

There is also a real meaning risk: no and gnaw, low and law, boat and bought, coat and caught.

A vowel that changes while you say it.

What your language doesTwo of the five usually need work

Italian, Spanish and most five-vowel languages have pure vowels, so a vowel is one steady sound from beginning to end. English asks you to move inside the vowel, which feels unnecessary.

Italian speakers especially flatten the o in no and home, producing a single sustained o, and flatten the a in day to a pure e.

Of the five, time, now and boy are often already fine, because many languages have similar sequences. The two that usually need work are no and day.

How to make itTwo vowels joined, then sped up

Say each one as two vowels joined together, then speed it up until it becomes one movement.

No is o gliding to oo, with your lips rounding as you go. Day is e gliding to ee, with your tongue rising. Time is ah gliding to ee. Now is ah gliding to oo. Boy is aw gliding to ee.

The picture: a car changing lanes. It does not jump. It moves across while continuing forward.

The way in. Say no in slow motion, taking two full seconds, and feel your lips round during the vowel. Then speed it up. The movement should still be there. On a mirror, your lips visibly change shape during the vowel. If they hold still, it is flat.

Why is a mirror useful for the vowel in no but not for the vowel in cut?

Because the vowel in no is made partly with your lips, and you can watch them move. The vowel in cut happens inside your mouth where you cannot see it.
