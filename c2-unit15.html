15 · Reporting and the passive

Attributing and shading responsibility.

ACCESS

Goal. Read the compliance briefing and notice the reporting choices in bold. Each verb or passive signals a stance toward the claim. You are spotting the pattern here, not producing it yet.

Nadia Where are we on the vendor issue?

Tom The vendor claims the breach was accidental.

Ruth But it is alleged that they knew for weeks.

Nadia And our side?

Tom We concede that our monitoring had a gap.

Ruth Officially, mistakes were made, but internally let's be precise about whose.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how reporting verbs and the passive encode your stance and manage responsibility. By the end you should be able to report a claim at exactly the distance you intend.

The structure

When you report what someone said, two choices quietly show your own attitude: the reporting verb and the voice. The verb is never neutral unless you make it so. “She says the figures are accurate” is flat, but “she claims” plants doubt, “she concedes” suggests a reluctant admission, and “she confirms” backs it up. The passive adds a second layer: “it is alleged that funds were misused” puts a claim at a distance and does not say who made it. The passive can also drop the actor completely, “mistakes were made,” which can be tactful or evasive depending on who deserves to know. At C2 you make these choices on purpose, so your report shows exactly how far you stand behind what you pass on.

Evaluative reporting verbs

Form
subject + reporting verb (claim, concede, admit, insist, allege, maintain, acknowledge) + that-clause
Meaning
the verb you choose colors how you regard the claim
Use it to
report what someone said while signaling your stance toward it
She claims the figures are accurate (doubt) vs she confirms them (endorse) vs she concedes they're off (reluctant).

Passive reporting for distance

Form
It + be + past participle + that; or subject + be + said / believed / alleged + to-infinitive
Meaning
attributes a claim to an unnamed source, distancing you from it
Use it to
report without naming who, or without vouching for the claim
It is alleged that funds were misused. / The vendor is believed to have missed the cutoff.

Passive for tone and focus

Form
be + past participle (+ by ...); choose what to make the subject
Meaning
lets you drop or delay the actor, shifting blame or focus
Use it to
soften, depersonalize, or foreground the affected party
Mistakes were made (agent hidden) vs The team made mistakes (agent named).

Watch out

The reporting verb is an editorial. “Admits,” “concedes,” “claims,” and “insists” all carry judgment; “says” and “states” are neutral. Choosing “claims” over “says” tells the reader you doubt it, whether you meant to or not.

The agentless passive can dodge accountability. “Errors were made” and “it was decided” hide who acted. That is legitimate for tact and evasive when the reader needs to know who is responsible. Don't hide the agent from someone entitled to it.

American / British English.

Reporting verb + “that.” Both varieties allow dropping “that” (“She said the figures are fine”); formal writing in both keeps it. This book keeps “that” in formal reporting.

Subjunctive after “insist / demand.” American “insisted that he be present” (subjunctive); British often “insisted that he should be present.” This book uses the subjunctive (see Unit 3).

The moves

Same claim, different distance. Keep the frame; change only the slot and the situation.

  • She claims that [the figures / the data] are accurate.
  • The vendor is alleged to [have missed / have breached] the terms.
  • He concedes that [the estimate / the plan] was optimistic.
  • It is widely believed that [the merger / the deal] will proceed.
  • Mistakes were made.

Where each move sits

Same claim, three stances. The reporting verb and the passive decide how far you stand from it.

Why this works

Here is how it works, and it is subtle enough that writers do it by accident all the time. A reader cannot hear your tone, so they judge your attitude from your word choice, and the reporting verb is the loudest signal you send. Write “the vendor claims the delay was unavoidable” and you have told the reader you doubt it, even without saying so; write “the vendor explains” and you have taken their side. That is an argument made without an argument, which is why “says” and “states” are the words to reach for when you truly want to stay neutral. The passive does the same for responsibility: “the team missed the deadline” points a finger; “the deadline was missed” quietly lowers it. Neither is dishonest by itself, but both are choices, so make them on purpose.

1 · Under pressure

The reporting verb is chosen in the second you open your mouth, and it commits you. Forty-five seconds each.

2 · Say it

Doubt leaks through the voice even when the verb is neutral. That leak is either a tool or an accident.

Record situation 1, then record it again with says instead of claims. Play both back. Listen for one thing: can you hear the doubt in the second one too? If you can, your voice is doing what “claims” does, and you need to decide whether you meant to.

3 · With a partner, if you have one

one of you reports the same piece of news three times: once endorsing it (“I can confirm...”), once neutrally (“She says...”), and once at arm's length (“It is alleged...”). Your partner names the stance each time and says how the verb or the voice gave it away. Then swap, this time practicing the passive: report a mistake first with the agent named and owned, then with the agent hidden, and discuss when each is honest and when it's a dodge.

Then, take a report you wrote recently and check every reporting verb and every passive. For each, ask: is this the stance I actually mean, or did I smuggle in doubt, or dodge responsibility, without deciding to? Rewrite any that don't say what you intend.
