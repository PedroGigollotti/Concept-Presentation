ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn the families of discourse markers, how placement shifts emphasis, and how they hold a long text together. By the end you should be able to signpost an argument precisely.

The structure

Discourse markers are the words that make the logic of a text visible, the “however,” “therefore,” “moreover,” “on balance” that tell a reader how one sentence connects to the last. At C2 the skill is no longer knowing what they mean but using the full range accurately and placing them well across a long text. They fall into groups by job: contrast (“however,” “nonetheless,” “that said”), addition (“moreover,” “furthermore”), result (“therefore,” “consequently”), and restating (“in other words,” “that is”). Where you put the marker matters as much as which one you choose: “However, we disagreed” sets the whole sentence against the last one, while “We disagreed, however, on the timing” narrows the contrast to “timing.” Used well, these turn a list of facts into an argument the reader can follow.

Connective adverbials, by function

Form
contrast (however, nonetheless), addition (moreover, furthermore), result (therefore, consequently, hence), sequence (subsequently, in turn)
Meaning
signals the logical relation between sentences
Use it to
make the shape of an argument visible to the reader
The pilot succeeded. However, the cost model didn't hold.

Placement and punctuation

Form
initial (However, ...), medial (The plan, however, ...), or final; always set off by commas
Meaning
where you place the marker shifts the emphasis and the rhythm
Use it to
control which word the contrast lands on, and vary the rhythm
However, we disagreed. vs We disagreed, however, on the timing. (medial narrows it to “timing”)

Pairs and long-range links

Form
correlatives and referring markers: on the one hand ... on the other; admittedly ... nonetheless; this / that / such linking back
Meaning
tie clauses and paragraphs together across distance
Use it to
hold a multi-paragraph argument together
Admittedly, the data is thin. Nonetheless, the trend is clear.

Watch out

“However” is not “but” with a comma. “However” joins sentences, not clauses: “We tried; however, it failed” or “We tried. However, it failed,” never “We tried, however it failed.” Use a period or a semicolon before it.

Don't signpost what's already clear. A marker on every sentence (“Moreover... Furthermore... In addition...”) clutters and condescends. Use them at the joints of the argument, not on every plank.

American / British English.

Punctuating “however.” Both varieties use a semicolon or period before a sentence-connecting “however”; American English is stricter about not using a comma there. This book uses the semicolon or period.

“While” and “whilst.” Both varieties use “while” and “whereas” for contrast; British English also uses “whilst.” This book uses “while” and “whereas.”

The moves

Same facts, now connected. Keep the frame; change only the slot and the situation.

  • That said, the risk remains.
  • Consequently, we paused the rollout.
  • The plan, however, has one flaw.
  • Admittedly, the data is thin.
  • On balance, it's worth doing.

Where each move sits

Same four facts, now an argument. The markers make the logic visible instead of leaving the reader to infer it.

Why this works

Here is why these small words carry so much weight. A reader does not just take in sentences; they build a running picture of how the sentences connect, and if you do not signal the connection, they have to guess it, which is slow and easy to get wrong. A discourse marker does that work for them: the moment they see “however,” they know a contrast is coming and get ready for it; “therefore” tells them a conclusion follows. Across a long document these signals add up to a clear shape. But the same words, overused, become noise. If every sentence starts with “moreover” or “furthermore,” they stop meaning anything and start sounding mechanical, and worse, they suggest the reader cannot see obvious links alone. So place them at the real turning points, and let the plain connections stand on their own.

1 · Under pressure

Markers are what turn four facts into an argument, and you usually have to do that out loud, in a debrief. Forty-five seconds each.

2 · Say it

Spoken markers need a pause around them or they disappear into the sentence.

Record situation 3. Play it back and listen for one thing: is there a beat after admittedly and after nonetheless? Those two words are signposts, and a signpost you drive past at speed has not signposted anything.

3 · With a partner, if you have one

one of you states four disconnected facts about a project, flat and unlinked. The other repeats them back as a coherent mini-argument, inserting discourse markers to show contrast, cause, and conclusion: “However...,” “Consequently...,” “On balance....” Then swap. The test: could a listener now draw the shape of your argument, where before they only had a list?

Then, take a short, choppy paragraph you wrote recently and add no more than two discourse markers, placed at the real joints of the argument. Resist adding a third. Notice that two well-placed markers do more than five scattered ones.
