14 · Speculation and deduction

Reading intent behind a message.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how modal verbs grade certainty from a loose guess to a near-sure conclusion, and how to read intent from limited evidence responsibly.

The structure

Reading intent from limited evidence is a skill of graded certainty, not a single guess. English marks the exact confidence of a deduction with modal verbs: might and could signal a loose possibility, must signals a conclusion you are nearly certain of based on evidence, and can't have signals a conclusion you are ruling out. Choosing the right modal tells your listener exactly how much weight to put on your read of the situation.

Present speculation

Form
might / could / may + base verb
Meaning
a loose possibility about the present situation
Use it to
raise a possibility without claiming to know
They might just be busy.

Past deduction

Form
must have / can't have + past participle
Meaning
a strong conclusion about the past, based on evidence
Use it to
state a near-certain or a ruled-out conclusion about something already done
They must have seen the email. They can't have forgotten, we followed up twice.

Watch out

Don't use must for the past without have. “They must saw the email” is wrong. Use must have seen.

Don't confuse mustn't have with can't have. For a ruled-out past conclusion, use can't have: they can't have forgotten. Mustn't have is not standard for this meaning.

American / British English.

May for present possibility. Both varieties use may for possibility, but American business writing tends to prefer might in speech as slightly less formal. British English uses may more freely in everyday conversation. This book uses might for the conversational register.

The moves

Same grading in every line. The highlighted slot changes; the modal sets exactly how sure you are.

  • They might just be [busy / traveling].
  • They must have [seen the email / gotten the note].
  • It could be a [budget issue / timing issue].
  • They can't have [forgotten / missed it], we followed up twice.
  • She may well [be waiting on approval / be reviewing internally].

Where each move sits

A loose guess sits on the left. A near-certain conclusion, backed by evidence, sits on the right.

Why this works

Guessing at someone else's intent is always a little risky, and modal verbs let you signal exactly how much you are willing to stake on the guess. Might costs you nothing if you are wrong. Must have is a real claim, backed by evidence, that invites the listener to act on it. Reach for a stronger modal only when you actually have evidence to justify it, since overclaiming certainty on a guess is exactly how a reasonable read of a situation turns into an unfair accusation.
