2 · Separable, inseparable, and three-part

Writing a clean sentence: the one syntax rule set, taught once.

ACCESS

Phrasal verbs have one syntax rule set, and ten minutes now saves confusion for the rest of the book. Some split around their object; most never do. One phrase here, live with, follows a pattern you'll learn to spot.

Two colleagues editing a document, cleaning up the word order one sentence at a time.

Nadia Small fix: you wrote "turn off it." It's "turn it off," the pronoun goes in the middle.

Sam Got it. And "look after the servers"? Can I say "look the servers after"?

Nadia No, that one never splits. "Look after them," always together.

Sam What about "we'll have to put up with the delay"?

Nadia Three-part verb, also glued. "Put up with it," never split. Same with "live with the risk."

Sam So separable ones split, the rest stay whole. Clear.

ANALYSIS

Phrasal verbs come in four shapes, and the only thing you have to learn is where the object can sit. Three of the four are easy: they never split. The one that splits, the separable transitive, has a single firm rule about pronouns.

Separable transitive verbs take an object that can go after the particle or between (turn off the light, turn the light off), but a pronoun must go between (turn it off, never turn off it). Inseparable transitive verbs keep the object after the particle, always (look after the kids, look after them). Intransitive verbs take no object at all (the deal fell through). Three-part verbs, verb plus particle plus preposition, are inseparable and keep the object at the end (put up with it, look forward to it).

Type Example Where the object goes
Separableturn offpronoun between: turn it off
Inseparablelook afterafter only: look after them
Intransitivebreak downno object: it broke down
Three-partput up withafter only: put up with it

Only the separable verbs split, and only with a firm rule for pronouns. The other three stay whole.

Watch out. The pronoun error is the most common one. "Can you fill out it?" should be "fill it out." Whenever the object is a pronoun and the verb is separable, the pronoun goes in the middle. No exceptions.
American / British English. Americans fill out a form; the British fill it in. Both are separable and both follow the pronoun rule: fill it out, fill it in. This book uses fill out.

This unit comes early because the syntax never changes. Every phrasal verb in the next thirty units obeys these four shapes. Once you know that separable verbs split, with the pronoun rule, and everything else stays whole, you can write any phrasal verb cleanly without thinking about it again.
