Professional English Practice · Phrasal Verbs

12 · ON: imposition, reliance, and scrutiny

Loading work onto others, leaning on people, and keeping watch.

ACCESS

Read the scene and notice the moves in bold. One of them, lean on, is never taught in this book. Before you read on, let the particle show you what it means.

A claims operation at an insurer, sorting who does what as volume climbs.

Ines Volume is up twenty percent. We can't push all of it on the same three adjusters.

Rafi Then pass the routine auto claims on to the junior team and keep the complex ones here.

Ines Fine, but we count on Dana to review anything over fifty thousand before it pays.

Rafi She's stretched. When she's out, we lean on the senior adjusters, but they're slammed too.

Ines Understood. Legal will keep on at us about the backlog, so check on the oldest files daily.

Rafi Got it. I'll sit on the disputed batch until the medical reports arrive. No point paying blind.

ANALYSIS

On is the same hub you met in Unit 11: in contact with a surface. This one takes the senses where the contact is pressure: weight placed onto a person, weight leaned on for support, or attention pressed onto something to watch it.

In the imposition sense, a burden is placed onto someone else: you pass a task on, foist an unwanted job on them. In the reliance sense, you put your weight on something and trust it to hold: count on, depend on, rely on. In the scrutiny sense, attention is pressed onto a thing to keep watch: check on, sit on, keep on at.

The same wheel as Unit 11, turned. This unit takes the three solid spokes; the dotted ones were Unit 11.

Watch out. Count on, depend on, rely on are close, not identical. Count on is personal and confident. Depend on can be neutral or needy. Rely on is formal and structural. And sit on has two senses: delay a decision, or be a member of a committee.
American / British English. Both varieties use pass on, rely on, and check on the same way. Keep on at someone, meaning to nag them persistently, is more distinctly British; American English usually says keep after someone instead.

Hold “on means weight placed in contact,” and the only question is which way the weight goes. Onto someone else, onto something you trust, or onto a thing you are watching. This is why pass it on can feel like relief or like dumping, depending on who catches it.
