ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how ellipsis omits recoverable words and how substitutes stand in for them, across sentences and turns. By the end you should be able to cut repetition without losing meaning.

The structure

Ellipsis and substitution are the two ways English avoids saying the same thing twice, and at C2 they work not just inside a sentence but across turns and paragraphs. Ellipsis simply leaves out words the reader can fill in: “She can code and design” drops the repeated she can; “I haven't finished, but I will” leaves the verb understood after will. Substitution puts a short stand-in where the full phrase would go: do or do so for a verb phrase, one or ones for a noun, so or not for a whole clause. Both save words and connect ideas at the same time, because leaving a gap or a stand-in makes the reader look back to what you already said, which is exactly what ties a text together.

Ellipsis: omit repeated words

Form
leave out words recoverable from context, especially after auxiliaries and in coordination
Meaning
avoids repeating what is already clear
Use it to
keep sentences and exchanges lean
She can code and design. / I haven't finished, but I will. / Who's coming? Marco is.

Substitution: use a pro-form

Form
do / do so / do it (verb phrase); one / ones (noun); so / not (clause); the same
Meaning
replaces a repeated element with a short stand-in
Use it to
refer back without repeating the phrase
Please review the draft. I'll do so. / I prefer the blue ones. / Will it ship? I hope so; I'm afraid not.

Cross-turn economy

Form
ellipsis and substitution reaching back across a turn or a sentence
Meaning
carries meaning forward without restating it
Use it to
make dialogue and connected prose flow
Did the vendor confirm? They did. / We considered three options and rejected two.

Watch out

Ellipsis needs an exact antecedent. You can only omit what the reader can reconstruct precisely, and the forms must match. She's taller than me works (am is recoverable); don't ellipt across a change of verb form, where the omitted word wouldn't actually fit.

Do so is formal; do it is neutral. “Please review it and do so by Friday” reads formally; in speech, do it is more natural. Do so replaces a whole verb phrase, not a noun; keep the register consistent.

American / British English.

Have vs do echo. British English: “Do you have one? Yes, I have.” American English: Yes, I do. This book uses “I do.”

Reduced infinitive. Both varieties leave the verb out after to (“I'd love to,” “You don't have to”); American English uses it freely. This book uses the reduced form.

The moves

Same meaning, fewer words. Keep the frame; change only the slot and the situation.

  • I haven't yet, but [I will / she will].
  • Please [do so / do it] by Friday.
  • I prefer the [blue / smaller] ones.
  • Will it ship? I [hope so / think so].
  • We kept the third and [dropped the rest / cut two].

Where each move sits

Same meaning, fewer words. Ellipsis drops the verb phrase the reader can already supply.

Why this works

Here is why the missing words help instead of hurting. Every time you drop a phrase or replace it with do so or one, you leave a small gap that the reader fills by looking back at what you already said, and that act of looking back is exactly what connection is. So saving words and joining ideas turn out to be the same move. “They asked us to present, so we did” is not only shorter than repeating we presented; the did points straight back to present and ties the two clauses together. There is one limit: the thing left out has to be clear and grammatically exact, or the gap traps the reader instead of guiding them. She's taller than me works because am is easy to supply; a missing or mismatched word leaves them stuck. Used cleanly, these two tools make writing both shorter and more tightly joined.

1 · Under pressure

Ellipsis is the fastest thing in this book, so this is the fastest set. Thirty seconds each. If you need longer, you are still building the full phrase in your head.

2 · Say it

Short replies are where non-native speakers most often sound formal by accident, because the full phrase is safer and slower.

Record all four in one take, quickly, as though answering someone standing at your desk. Play it back. Listen for one thing: did you speed up? Ellipsis is not just shorter, it is faster. If the four clipped replies took as long as four full sentences, the economy is on the page only.

3 · With a partner, if you have one

hold a rapid question-and-answer about a project (“Did it ship?” “Will they sign?” “Can you review it?”), but every answer must use ellipsis or a substitute, never a full repeated phrase: They did, I hope so, I'd be happy to. If either of you repeats a phrase you could have left out, you lose the round. Then swap roles.

Then, take a short exchange or paragraph you wrote recently and cut every repeated phrase you can, replacing it with ellipsis, do so, one, so, or not. Read it aloud and check that nothing important got lost, only the words the reader could already supply.
