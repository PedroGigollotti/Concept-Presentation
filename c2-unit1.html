1 · Continuous and perfect aspect

Coloring a message without stating it.

ACCESS

Goal. Read the client check-in and notice the moves in bold. Every one uses aspect to manage tone, not to mark time. You are spotting the pattern here, not producing it yet.

Client I need to know today whether the audit will be ready by Friday.

Ana Understood. We've been going through the figures line by line since Monday, so we're close.

Client Close isn't a date.

Ana Fair. I was hoping we could confirm the exact date once Marco wraps the reconciliation this afternoon.

Marco I've been meaning to flag one thing: two entries don't tie out, and I'd rather fix them than rush past it.

Ana Will you be sending the clean version to the client by five, Marco?

Client All right. Keep me posted.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how continuous and perfect aspect shift a message's stance, and when each shift helps. By the end you should be able to explain the effect and choose it on purpose.

The structure

Every verb in English can show time in more than one way; this is called aspect. The continuous form (be + -ing) shows an action in progress, as in I am writing. The perfect form (have + past participle) links a past action to now, as in I have written. At B2 you learned these forms to talk about time. At C2 you use them for something more: to change how a message feels. The same verb, in a different form, can make a request sound softer, make a criticism sound less harsh, or make a piece of work sound like it is still going on. The grammar is the same as before. What is new is that you now choose the form for its tone, not only for its time.

Continuous for a provisional stance

Form
be + -ing (often past: I was hoping, I was wondering; or present: you're being)
Meaning
frames an action or state as temporary, in progress, or not yet fixed
Use it to
soften a request, an opinion, or a criticism so it feels less absolute
I was hoping we could revisit the timeline. (softer and more tentative than “I hope we can”)

Perfect for engagement and open ends

Form
have + -en, and especially have been + -ing
Meaning
links a past action to the present, foregrounding ongoing relevance or effort over a finished result
Use it to
signal continued work, manage the impression you leave, or keep a matter live
We've been looking into your request. (still in progress, still live, unlike we looked into it, which closes it)

Future continuous for the non-imposing future

Form
will be + -ing
Meaning
presents a future event as expected and routine rather than requested
Use it to
ask or confirm without pressure, presuming nothing of the other person
Will you be sending the deck today? (a neutral check-in, where “Will you send the deck today?” can read as a demand)

Watch out

A softener used everywhere stops softening. If every line is “I was just wondering if maybe we could,” you sound unsure and lose authority. Reserve the continuous softener for the moments that genuinely need it.

State verbs still resist the continuous. “I was hoping” and I'm thinking are established and idiomatic, but “I am knowing” or “I am understanding” are not. The stance uses are a fixed set, not a free license to add -ing.

American / British English.

Present perfect with just, already, and yet. American English often uses the past simple (I already sent it; Did you send it yet?), while British English keeps the present perfect (I've already sent it; Have you sent it yet?). This book teaches the present perfect, because its still relevant now meaning is exactly what carries the stance.

Past participle of get. American we've gotten nowhere; British we've got nowhere. This book uses gotten.

The moves

Same aspect, different jobs. Keep the frame; change only the slot and the situation.

  • I was hoping [we could revisit / you might reconsider] this.
  • We've been [looking into / working on] the issue.
  • You're being [cautious / thorough] here.
  • Will you be [joining us / sending it] today?
  • I've been meaning to [raise / flag] this.

Where each move sits

The words barely change. The aspect decides how fixed, or how provisional, the message feels.

Why this works

Compare two sentences. “I hope you can review this” sounds like a fixed expectation. “I was hoping you could review this” sounds softer and more open, even though the facts are the same. The form was hoping makes the request feel less firm, so the other person feels free to say no. The same works in reverse. “We looked into it” sounds finished and closed. “We have been looking into it” sounds like the work is still going on. Readers notice this difference quickly, without thinking about grammar rules, so a small change to the verb form lets you sound polite or firm without changing any of the words. One warning: if you soften every sentence, the softening stops working and you begin to sound unsure. Use it only when the moment truly needs it.

1 · Under pressure

Aspect works or fails in the second you need it, not in the minute you spend choosing it. Each situation gives you forty seconds. Say your line out loud first, then type it. The model appears when the clock runs out, so there is nothing to be gained by waiting.

2 · Say it

Now say one of them properly. Aspect carries tone, and tone lives in the voice before it lives on the page: I was hoping only sounds tentative if you say it that way. Record yourself, play it back, and listen for one thing, whether you sound like someone who could be told no.

Record the softened request from situation 2, then play it back. Ask yourself: does it sound like a genuine offer to be refused, or like a demand wearing polite words? If it is the second, say it again more slowly and land the stress on hoping, not on you.

3 · With a partner, if you have one

Role-play a status update to a demanding stakeholder. One of you plays the stakeholder pushing for a firm answer; the other keeps the issue live and the tone level, using the perfect continuous (we've been...) and a continuous softener (I was hoping...). Swap roles and run it again, and this time slip in one piece of mild criticism using you're being... so it lands as behavior rather than character.

Then, take three blunt lines from your own recent messages and rewrite each one so the aspect changes its stance, one softened request, one still in progress, and one temporary-behavior reframe. Read the pairs aloud until the softer version feels as natural to say as the blunt one did.
