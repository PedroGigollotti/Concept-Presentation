3 · Stress, rhythm, and hearing them

Following a fast meeting: the stress sits on the particle, so train your ear.

ACCESS

This one's for your ears, not your grammar. In speech, phrasal verbs hide: the stress lands on the little particle, and the words blur together. One phrase, sound out, you may not have met. Predict it, then read on.

A fast-moving planning meeting. One person keeps losing the phrasal verbs in the blur.

Rafa We'll push the launch back a week and roll it out after QA signs off.

Sana Sorry, push it back? I heard push the launch and lost the rest.

Rafa Push it BACK. The stress is on back; that's how you catch the whole verb.

Sana Got it. My ear skips the little words, but those little words are the verb.

Rafa Exactly. Same when I say we'll sound the client out first.

Sana Sound them out, feel out their reaction. I caught out that time.

ANALYSIS

Phrasal verbs are hard to hear, not hard to understand. Two things happen in fast speech. First, the stress lands on the particle, not the base verb: you say turn it OFF, look it UP, push it BACK. The base verb is quiet; the particle that changes its meaning is loud. Second, the unstressed words shrink and blend, pick them up becomes pickem UP, get out of it becomes get OUTTA it.

For a non-native listener, this is a trap. Your ear was trained to treat up, out, and off as small, skippable prepositions, so it tunes them out, exactly the words carrying the stress and half the meaning. The fix is counterintuitive: listen for the stressed little word.

On the page the particle looks small. In your ear it's the loudest beat in the phrase. Listen for it.

Watch out. Catch the stress first, not the meaning. Learners try to translate each word as it lands and fall behind. Instead, let the stressed particle flag the phrasal verb, then the sense follows.
American / British English. American English blends harder: gonna, wanna, gotta, lemme. British English reduces too but keeps crisper consonants. Both put the stress on the particle. This book teaches the American reductions.

Listening gets its own unit because comprehension, not production, is where phrasal verbs actually break down. Retrain the ear deliberately. The little words you were taught to skip, up, out, off, back, in, are the stressed, meaning-bearing heart of spoken English.

Meet a Stranger

These three are not taught in this book, and all three are about sound. Reason from the particle, then reveal the key.

1. “She rattled off the numbers before I could write them down.”

2. “The AC drowned out half of what he said.”

3. “His voice trailed off at the end of the call.”

rattle off: to say something quickly from memory. Off: out in a rapid stream.

drown out: to make a sound impossible to hear. Out: overpowered and lost.

trail off: to fade gradually into silence. Off: drifting away to nothing.
