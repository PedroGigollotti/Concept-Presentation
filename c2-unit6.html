6 · Comparative phrases and clauses

Reporting figures that barely differ.

ACCESS

Goal. Read the results review and notice the comparisons in bold. Each one sizes a difference precisely, neither inflating nor shrinking it. You are spotting the pattern here, not producing it yet.

Lena Give me the quarter in one line.

Omar Revenue was no higher than Q2, essentially flat.

Grace And costs were marginally higher, up under a point.

Lena So what happened to margin?

Omar The dip was so slight as to be immaterial.

Grace One real change: churn is nowhere near as high as we feared.

Lena Good. The tighter we run onboarding, the lower it goes.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn the marked comparatives, the degree modifiers, and the comparative clauses that let you report a difference at its true size. By the end you should be able to size any gap precisely.

The structure

At C1 you compared things to make a point stronger. At C2 you compare to be exact, especially when two numbers are close and it would be easy to turn a small difference into drama. Three tools do most of this work. The first is a set of comparatives that limit a gap: “no higher than,” “little more than,” “nowhere near as large as,” which say precisely how far something did, or did not, move. The second is the degree word, “marginally,” “slightly,” “substantially,” “far,” which sizes a difference honestly. The third links size to a result: “so slight as to be immaterial,” “the more we tested, the fewer we shipped.” Together they let you report a difference at its true size, no bigger and no smaller.

Marked comparatives that cap a gap

Form
no / not any + comparative + than; no more than; little more than; nowhere near as ... as
Meaning
limits or denies a difference, often against expectation
Use it to
state precisely that one figure has not exceeded another
Revenue was no higher than last quarter. (level or below, and pointedly not up)

Degree modifiers for precision

Form
marginally, fractionally, appreciably, substantially, negligibly, nowhere near + comparative
Meaning
calibrates exactly how large the difference is
Use it to
report a gap without overstating or understating it
Costs were marginally higher, up less than one percent. (not “substantially higher”)

Marked comparative and result clauses

Form
so + adjective + as to; such + noun + that; the + comparative ..., the + comparative
Meaning
links a degree to its consequence, or ties two scales together
Use it to
express proportion, or a degree strong enough to cause a result, formally
The delay was so slight as to be immaterial. / The more we automated, the fewer errors we saw.

Watch out

“No higher” is not “not higher.” “No higher than X” means at most X, level or below, and signals “contrary to expectation, it did not rise.” “Not higher than X” merely denies exceeding X. Use “no + comparative” when the point is that it held or fell.

Match the modifier to the size. Don't call a one-percent change “substantially higher” or a forty-percent jump “marginally higher.” The degree adverb is a factual claim; choose the true one.

American / British English.

Different from / than / to. American English uses “different from” or “different than”; British English prefers “different from” or “different to,” and avoids “than.” This book uses “different from.”

Half again as large. American “half again as large” means 1.5 times; British English says “half as large again.” This book uses the American “half again as.”

The moves

Same rise, different size. Keep the frame; change only the slot and the situation.

  • no [higher / larger] than last quarter.
  • marginally [higher / slower], by under a point.
  • so [slight / small] as to be immaterial.
  • nowhere near as [costly / severe] as forecast.
  • the more [we automate / we test], the fewer the errors.

Where each move sits

Same rise, five sizes. The comparative you choose is a factual claim about how big the gap is.

Why this works

Here is why precise comparison is really about trust. Every size word you attach to a number is a claim about that number. Call a one-percent rise “a sharp increase” and a careful reader quietly starts to doubt your other figures. Call a forty-percent overrun “a slight uptick” and you have misled them, whether you meant to or not. The limiting comparatives go further: “no higher than last year” does not just mean “the same,” it means “and, in case you expected a rise, there wasn't one.” Learn the frames as fixed patterns so the exact option comes as easily as the dramatic one. In professional writing, the person who sizes differences accurately is the person whose numbers get believed.

1 · Under pressure

Sizing a gap is a judgment you make in the moment someone asks for the number. Forty seconds each. The word has to match the figure.

2 · Say it

Precision sounds like confidence, and vagueness sounds like hedging, even when the numbers are the same.

Record situations 1 and 2 one after the other. Play them back and listen for one thing: does your voice change between them? It should. If “marginally” and “substantially” come out at the same weight, the listener will trust the adverb less than they trust your tone.

3 · With a partner, if you have one

one of you reads out five invented figures (this quarter versus last), and the other reports each change aloud, choosing the degree word that fits the real size: “marginally higher,” “no higher than,” “substantially up,” “nowhere near as high.” Then swap. The rule is simple: the word must match the number, and if your partner thinks you over- or under-stated a gap, they challenge it.

Then, find a comparison you made recently (“a bit more,” “way faster,” “basically the same”) and rewrite it with a precise degree word and, where you can, an actual figure. Notice how much more credible the precise version sounds.
