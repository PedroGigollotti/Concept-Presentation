30 · Phrasal verbs in writing

Email, chat, and documentation: where they work, and where to switch.

ACCESS

Writing is not one register; it's a ladder. A phrasal verb that's perfect in a chat can be too loose in a contract, and the belief that formal writing bans phrasal verbs is a myth. Watch where each choice lands.

Two colleagues turning one set of notes into a chat message, a client email, and a formal statement of work.

Amir The chat version's easy: "we'll kick off Monday and roll it out in phases."

Nadia In the client email, keep "roll out," and "kick off" is fine too. It's warm, not sloppy.

Amir And the statement of work?

Nadia There, "kick off" becomes "commence." But "carry out the audit" stays. Good writing doesn't ban phrasal verbs, it places them.

Amir So it's not phrasal-or-not. It's which register the document sits in.

Nadia Exactly. "Follow up" works everywhere. "Sort out" I'd write as "resolve" in the contract, and leave as is in the email.

ANALYSIS

Speech had one rule: default to the phrasal verb. Writing is a ladder with three rungs, chat, email, and formal document, and the right choice depends on the rung. The myth is that formal writing bans phrasal verbs. It does not. It bans the wrong ones in the wrong place.

Chat and instant message are almost speech. Email is the wide middle, warm and professional. Formal documents are where some phrasal verbs give way to their Latinate twins for precision, while others, carry out, set out, roll out, follow up, are so standard they stay.

The same meaning climbs the ladder. The bottom rows barely change; some phrasal verbs are at home even in a contract.

Watch out. The myth, stated plainly: no credible style guide bans phrasal verbs from formal writing. What weak writing overuses is vague phrasal verbs where a precise verb exists. And don't strand a long object: keep the particle near the verb when the object is long.
American / British English. American business writing uses reach out to for contact; some British readers find it over-friendly and prefer contact or get in touch. This book uses get in touch in formal writing, reach out in email.

Writing judges register by document, not by sentence. The same verb that's perfect in a chat can be too casual in a signed contract, and the reader feels the mismatch instantly. This is why the myth is worth killing outright: the goal was never to avoid phrasal verbs, it was to place the right one on the right rung.

set out — to state or present something formally. Out: laid out in the open.

draw up — to prepare a formal document. Up: drawn up into finished form.

enter into — to formally begin an agreement. Into: crossing into a binding commitment.
