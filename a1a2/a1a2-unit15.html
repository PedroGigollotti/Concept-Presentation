Adjectives before nouns and after be

In front of the thing, or after the verb.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Two positions, one form, and the comma between two of them. By the end you can describe anything you can point at.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

In front of the noun, or after the verb?

Both are correct and they say different things. In front of the noun the word only helps to name it: a small cafe. After be the word is what you are saying about it: The cafe is small. One of those is a sentence and the other is not.

THE FORM

in front of nouns →adjective + nouna new cafe
after be →be + adjectiveIt's small.
more than one thing →the adjective does not changesmall, friendly places
with a plural verb →still one formThe staff are friendly.
two of them together →a comma betweenI love small, friendly places.
never →an -s on the adjectivetwo cheap coffees

Two positions, and both are right

A describing word has exactly two places to stand in English: in front of nouns, or after be. The scene uses both in one line.

In front of the noun: a new cafe. It goes after a or the and before the noun, with nothing between it and the noun.

After be: It's small. Here the word is the thing you are saying, and the sentence is finished.

So decide first whether you are naming a thing or saying something about it. Name it, and the word goes in front. Say something, and it goes after the verb.

The word never changes, and that is the hard part

In your own language this word changes for one or many, and for a man or a woman. In English it does neither, ever.

One cafe is small. Two cafes are small. A man is friendly and a woman is friendly.

Never “two smalls cafes” and never “a friendlys place”. The -s belongs to nouns and to nothing else.

So write the noun first if it helps, and put the -s on it. Only then put the describing word in front, with nothing added to it.

After be, the verb still has to agree

The word itself does not change, but the verb in front of it does, and learners drop it because nothing else in the sentence moved.

Mia says “The staff are friendly.” The people are more than one, so the verb is are. The word after it is exactly as it was.

One cafe: The cafe is cheap. Two cafes: The cafes are cheap. Only the verb moved.

So count the thing, choose the verb from unit 1, and leave the last word alone.

Two of them together take a comma

Sam ends the conversation with two of these words in a row, and there is a comma between them: small, friendly places.

The comma is there because the two words are equal. Neither one is closer to the noun than the other, and both are true of the place.

You will meet lists of rules about the order of three or four of them. You do not need those yet, and two is what people say.

So keep it to two, put a comma between them, and say the more important one first.

Watch out

The word takes no -s, ever. Say “two small cafes.” Never “two smalls cafes.” The -s is on the noun and nowhere else.

It goes in front of the noun, not after it. Say “a new cafe.” Never “a cafe new.” Your own language puts it after. English does not.

After be, the verb still counts the thing. Say “The staff are friendly.” Never “The staff is friendly.”

The moves

Decide whether you are naming it or saying something about it. The frame does not change; only the blue words do.

naming the thing

  • a [clean, cozy] [room, place]
  • the [fast, slow] [train, bus]
  • [friendly, loud] [staff, neighbors]
  • a [heavy, light] [box, bag]

saying something about it

  • The [room, place] is [clean, cozy].
  • The [trains, buses] are [fast, slow].
  • The staff [are] [friendly, loud].
  • This [box, bag] is [heavy, light].

The same word in the two places it can stand. Naming a thing on the left, saying something about it on the right.

Why this works

Everything you say about a place, a person or a thing runs through these words. They are also the fastest vocabulary you will ever learn, because each one arrives with its opposite. The grammar costs almost nothing. The one thing to fight is the -s: your own language puts it there and English never does.
