ANALYSIS

Goal. Count it before you say it, and the word in front comes out right every time. By the end you can name one thing and more than one thing.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

One, or more than one?

Count it before you say it. If it is one, put a in front, or an when the next word starts with a vowel sound. If it is more than one, add -s to the word and put nothing in front of it.

THE FORM

one thing →aa laptop
one thing, vowel sound →anan eraser
more than one →add -spens
after s, ch, sh or x →add -esboxes
and in front of it →nothing at allThey're pens.
four words →a new wordmen, women, children, people

One thing takes a word in front

In English you almost never name one thing on its own. You put a small word in front of it: a laptop, a headset, an eraser.

This is the rule to slow down for, because your own language may not have it. Many languages name a job with nothing in front of it. English never does.

So say “I'm a nurse.” and “She's a manager.” A job in the singular always has the small word.

One thing to watch: it does not go in front of my, your, his or her. Say “my chair”, never “a my chair”.

a or an, and the sound decides

Say the word out loud and listen to how it starts. That is the whole of it.

If it starts with an a, e, i, o or u sound, use an: an email, an engineer, an office, an old chair.

If it starts with any other sound, use a: a chair, a key, a box, a manager.

Look at the word right after it, not at the thing you are naming. It is a chair and an old chair, and the chair did not change.

One word to know now: an hour. The h is silent, so the word begins with an ow sound. Your ear is right and the spelling is not.

More than one adds -s

Count it. If it is more than one, add -s to the end: one chair, two chairs; one key, two keys.

After the sounds s, ch, sh and x, add -es instead. The word needs the extra beat: one box, two boxes; one watch, two watches.

And now the small word goes. Say “They're nurses.”, never “They're a nurses.”

So the two halves are one question with two answers. Ask how many, then either put a in front or put -s on the end.

Four words that do not add -s

Four words you will use every day change instead of adding -s. Learn them as four pairs and you are finished with them.

one man → two men. one woman → two women. one child → two children. one person → two people.

Use people, not persons. Say “Our team is five people.”

Everything else in this unit still holds for them: one child takes a in front, and two children take nothing.

Watch out

A job in the singular always takes a or an. Say “It's a laptop.” Never “It's laptop.” Your own language may name a thing with nothing in front of it. English does not.

More than one takes nothing in front. Say “They're pens.” Never “They're a pens.” The small word goes the moment there is more than one.

The sound decides, and the spelling does not. Say “an hour”: the h is silent, so the word begins with an ow sound. Say “a manager”: the m is not silent.

The moves

Count it, then say it. The frame does not change; only the blue words do.

one

  • I'm [a, an] [nurse, engineer].
  • It's [a, an] [box, email].
  • My [chair, watch] is old.
  • It's [a, an] old [chair, watch].

more than one

  • They're [chairs, boxes].
  • My [keys, emails] are here.
  • Two [men, women] are in our room.
  • [Nurses, Designers] are busy people.

Count first. One takes the small word in front; more than one takes the ending instead.

Why this works

There is no way round this one. You cannot name a job, a thing or a person in English without deciding how many there are. The decision shows up in two places at once: in front of the word, and on the end of it. Get it wrong and every sentence you say carries the mistake. The good news is that it is one question, asked once, before you open your mouth. Count it. Then say it.

Not graded. This is where the form stops being a rule and becomes a sentence you say.

Name four things on your desk, one at a time. Start each one with It's a or It's an. Say the word out loud first and let your ear pick.
