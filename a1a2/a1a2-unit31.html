Present perfect vs past simple

Announce it, then give the details.

ANALYSIS

Goal. The order the two tenses come in, the word that forces the switch, and the two questions that ask for each one.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Am I naming the moment?

If you are, use unit 22's tense. If you are not, use unit 29's. That is the same rule as unit 29 and it settles every sentence in this unit. I've been to New York names no moment; I went last spring names one.

THE FORM

the announcement →have + third formI've been to New York!
the first detail →unit 22's tenseI went last spring.
every detail after it →unit 22's tenseI stayed for a week.
asking about a life →Have you + third formHave you seen a Broadway show?
asking about a moment →When did + person + bare verbWhen did you go?
naming which one →unit 22's tenseYes, I saw Hamilton.

Announce in one tense, tell in the other

The two tenses are not alternatives. They come in an order, and the order is the same every time somebody tells you news.

The announcement has no date in it: “I've been to New York!” It says the thing happened in his life.

The details do have dates, so they switch: “I went last spring. I stayed for a week.”

So open with the perfect and switch as soon as you say when. Say the two sentences in that order and the tenses look after themselves.

Answer in the tense of the question

Two different questions are being asked in this scene, and each one names its own tense.

Have you seen...? asks about a whole life, and the short answer stays in that tense: Yes, I have.

But the moment you add which show, you are naming an occasion: “Yes, I saw Hamilton.”

So listen to the question, answer in its tense, and switch as soon as you add a detail of your own.

When did asks for a moment, and only that

One question word forces unit 22's tense every time it appears, and it is the commonest follow-up in English.

Mia asks “When did you go?” The question itself names a moment, so the answer must too.

You can never ask “When have you gone?” The two words fight each other.

So use When did for a moment and Have you ever for a life. They are two different questions.

Always and never keep the perfect

A few words are part of the life half, and a sentence with one of them in it stays in the perfect.

Mia ends the conversation with one: “I've always wanted to go.” No date, and none is possible.

The same for never, ever, twice and recently: they measure a life, not an occasion.

So keep those words in the perfect and put a date nowhere near them.

The idea

Watch out

A date forces the other tense, every time. Say “I went last spring.” Never “I've gone last spring.”

When did, never when have. Say “When did you go?” Never “When have you gone?”

Naming which one is a detail. Say “I saw Hamilton.” Never “I've seen Hamilton last night.”

The moves

Announce, then give the detail. The frame does not change; only the blue words do.

the announcement

  • I've [been, seen] [to New York, a show].
  • She's [changed, started] [jobs, a new job].
  • [Have] you [been, seen] it?
  • I've [always, never] wanted to go.

the details after it

  • I [went, stayed] [last spring, for a week].
  • I [saw, remembered] [the show, every detail].
  • [When] did you [go, start]?
  • It [was, looked] [amazing, perfect].

The announcement has no date. The first date in the conversation is where the tense changes.

Why this works

This is the shape of every piece of news you will ever tell in English: one sentence to announce it, and then the details. The order matters more than any single sentence, because a learner who opens with the past simple is answering a question nobody asked.

H

Over to you

Not graded. This is where the form stops being a rule and becomes a sentence you say.

Announce three things from your life, one sentence each, with no date in any of them.

Now give two details for each one. Every detail has a date, and every detail is in the other tense.
