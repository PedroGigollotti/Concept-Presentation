ANALYSIS

Goal. One shape after three verbs, one -ing that is not the one from unit 13, and the questions you will actually be asked at a first lunch.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

What shape does the second verb take?

Always -ing, after all three of these verbs, for every person. I like cooking. She loves swimming. They hate running. The first verb changes for the person; the second one never changes at all.

THE FORM

I, you, we, they →like / love / hate + verb-ingI love cooking.
he, she, it →likes / loves / hates + verb-ingShe hates running.
to ask →Do / Does + person + like + verb-ingDo you like cooking?
to say no →don't / doesn't + like + verb-ingNo, I don't.
the second verb →never changescooking, swimming, running
never →the bare verb after these threeI hate washing up.

After these three verbs, one shape

Three verbs say how you feel about an activity: like, love and hate. What follows them always ends in -ing.

Sam says “I love cooking” and “I hate washing up.” Two feelings, two activities, one shape.

The spelling of the ending is unit 13's, unchanged: swim → swimming, dance → dancing, read → reading.

So put the ending on before you say the sentence. Never “I love cook.”

The first verb still takes the person

Nothing from units 8 and 9 was suspended here, and that is what makes this unit different from unit 16.

Mia says “My brother likes cooking.” Sam says “My sister loves swimming.” The -s is on the first verb.

That is where it has always been, and nothing in this unit moves it.

To ask, the helper comes back: “Do you like cooking?” To say no, the same: “No, I don't.”

So build the sentence in two halves. Get the first verb right for the person, then put the ending on the second.

This -ing is not the one from unit 13

You have just spent a unit putting -ing on verbs, and this is a different job for the same ending.

In unit 13 it was half of a verb: I am cooking, and it could not appear without be in front of it.

Here it is the name of an activity. Cooking is the thing Sam loves, and it stands where a thing would stand.

So ask what the word is doing in the sentence. If it names the activity you feel something about, no be goes anywhere near it.

Answering, and agreeing

These three verbs generate a whole conversation, and it has a shape you can learn in one minute.

The question is unit 9's: “Do you like cooking?” The short answer is unit 9's too: “Yes, I do. No, I don't.”

To agree, say me too. Mia says exactly that, and it is the shortest useful sentence in this book.

So answer short first, then add the activity you do like. That is what both of them do, in every line.

Watch out

The second verb always takes the ending. Say “I love cooking.” Never “I love cook.” Your own language uses the bare verb here. English does not.

The -s stays on the first verb. Say “She loves swimming.” Never “She love swimming.”

No be in front of these three. Say “I love cooking.” Never “I am loving cooking.”

The moves

Say how you feel, then name the activity. The frame does not change; only the blue words do.

what you feel about it

  • I [love, like] [cooking, reading].
  • She [hates, likes] [running, waiting].
  • We [love, hate] [dancing, traveling].
  • My [brother, sister] [likes, hates] swimming.

asking, and answering short

  • [Do] you like [cooking, dancing]?
  • [Does] she like [reading, swimming]?
  • No, [I don't, she doesn't].
  • Yes, [I do, she does].

Two verbs in a row. The first one takes the person; the second one always takes the ending.

Why this works

This is the first conversation anybody has with you at a lunch table, and it runs entirely on three verbs. The pattern is also the cheapest grammar in the book: one ending and no exceptions. It works with every activity you will ever want to name.

Over to you

Not graded. This is where the form stops being a rule and becomes a sentence you say.
