Have

What you have, and what he has.

ANALYSIS

Goal. One verb, and the two things the learner's own language will not expect: the letter for one person, and the helper in every question and every no.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Is this the verb, or the helper?

On its own it is the verb: I have a bike. For one person it takes the letter: She has a car. To ask and to say no it uses do and does, the same as work and live. Nothing about it is special.

THE FORM

I, you, we, they →haveI have a bike.
he, she, it →hasMy sister has the good car.
to say no →don't / doesn't + haveMy car doesn't have air conditioning.
to ask →Do / Does + person + haveDoes it have a radio?
the short answer →person + do / doesYes, it does.
never →the verb on its own in a questionDo you have a car?

One letter, and you have the whole verb

There is nothing new to learn here. I, you, we, they take have. He, she, it and a name take has. That is the whole of it.

Ana says “I have an old one” about herself and “my sister has the good car” about one other person. Same verb, one letter apart.

The word is irregular only in its spelling. Have does not become haves; it becomes has. Learn the one word and you are finished.

Use it for what somebody owns, for what a thing contains, and for the people in a family: I have a bike. The car has a radio. She has two brothers.

To ask and to say no, the helper comes back

This is the half your own language will fight. In many languages the verb asks on its own. In American English it does not.

Sam asks “Do you have a car?” and “Does it have a radio?” The helper is there both times.

And the verb after it is bare. Never an -s on it, however much the sentence seems to want one.

Ana says “My car doesn't have air conditioning.” Same thing: the helper says no, and have keeps the bare form.

So build the sentence in this order: the helper first, then the person, then have with nothing on the end of it. Say the helper out loud before you say the verb, and the verb will come out bare on its own.

Never “Have you a car?” and never “Does it has a radio?” One letter in the sentence, on the helper, exactly as in unit 9.

The short answer uses the helper, not the verb

Somebody asks you a yes-or-no question with this verb, and the answer is not the verb.

“Do you have a car?” “Yes, I do.” “Does it have a radio?” “Yes, it does.”

Never “Yes, I have.” The helper asked, so the helper answers.

Say the short answer first, and only then say more. Ana does exactly that: “Yes, I do. I have an old one.”

What this verb is not for

Two jobs this word does in English are not in this unit, and knowing that now will save you a wrong sentence later.

It is not for what you eat or drink. Have lunch and have a coffee are a different use, and they come later.

It is not for what you must do. Have to is obligation, and it is a different verb in every way that matters.

Here it means one thing only: what somebody owns, or what a thing contains. Keep it to that and nothing will go wrong.

Watch out

One person takes has, and it is not spelled haves. Say “She has a car.” Never “She haves a car.” and never “She have a car.”

Ask with the helper, not with the verb. Say “Do you have a car?” Never “Have you a car?” Your own language asks with the verb alone. American English brings the helper.

Answer with the helper too. Say “Yes, I do.” Never “Yes, I have.”

The moves

Ask who owns it, then say it. The frame does not change; only the blue words do.

what you have

  • I [have] [a bike, a wallet].
  • She [has] [a car, a phone].
  • My [brother, sister] [has] a garage.
  • We [have] [two keys, one car].

asking, and saying it is missing

  • [Do] you have [a car, a bike]?
  • [Does] it have [a radio, a garage]?
  • My car [doesn't] have [a radio, air conditioning].
  • Yes, [I do, it does].

On its own it is the verb and it takes the letter. With the helper it is bare, and the helper has the letter.

American / British English

This book is American English. American English says I have a car and asks Do you have a car?

British English also says I've got a car and asks Have you got a car? You will hear it constantly, and it means the same thing. It is not taught here, and you never need it: the American form is understood everywhere.

Why this works

This is the verb you will need on your first day anywhere: what you have, what the room has, what the company has. And it is the verb where the helper feels most wrong, because the word is too common to seem to need one. Say Do you have ten times and the feeling goes away. Say Have you a car? once and an American will understand you and know exactly where you learned English.

H

Over to you

Not graded. This is where the form stops being a rule and becomes a sentence you say.

Say four things you have. Start each one with I have, and use a word from the list above.

Now say four about one other person, with has. Say the name first, then the verb.

Ask your partner six questions with Do you have and Does he have. Never start one with the verb.

In pairs. Answer each one with the short answer first: Yes, I do. No, it doesn't. Then say one more thing.
