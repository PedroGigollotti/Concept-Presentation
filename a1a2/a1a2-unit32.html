ANALYSIS

Goal. One question for the form, one word for the second half, two exceptions, and the way to say two things are the same.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

How long is the word?

Adjectives of one or two syllables take -er on the end: cheaper, faster, bigger, lighter. Three or more take more in front: more expensive, more comfortable, more dangerous. Count before you choose.

THE FORM

a short word →add -erThis one is cheaper.
a long word →more in frontThe blue one is more expensive.
ending in -y →-ierThe keyboard is easier.
good and bad →better and worseBut it's better.
the second half →thanIt's lighter than my old one.
the same →as ... asThis one is as fast as that one.

Count the syllables, then choose

One question gives you the shape, and it has nothing to do with meaning. Say the adjective out loud and count.

Adjectives of one or two syllables take -er: cheap → cheaper, fast → faster, light → lighter.

A short adjective ending in one vowel and one consonant doubles it: big → bigger. A word ending in -y takes -ier: easy → easier.

Three syllables or more take more in front: more expensive, more comfortable, more dangerous. So count, then write.

Than joins the two halves

A comparison has two sides and the second one does not arrive by itself. One word brings it.

Mia says “It's lighter than my old one.” Without that word the listener is still waiting.

Your own language may use a word that also means that or of. In English it is only ever than.

So say the word every time you compare. Never “lighter that my old one” and never “lighter of my old one”.

Two words break the rule

Almost every word follows the count. Two do not, and they are two of the commonest words in the language.

good becomes better. Sam says “True, but it's better.”

bad becomes worse. Neither takes -er and neither takes more.

So learn those two as words, and use the count for everything else. Never “more better”.

As ... as says they are the same

When the two things are equal, the comparison changes shape and no ending is added to anything.

Put the plain word between two as: This one is as fast as that one.

For a difference between two adjectives, put not in front of the first one: This one is not as fast as that one.

So use than when one is more and as ... as when they are the same. The word in the middle never changes.

As ... as says they are the same

The idea
The plain word between two as, with nothing added to it.
the two shapes
faster than that one · as fast as that one
from your own week
as safe as the old model
never
as faster as
This one is as fast as that one.

Watch out

One shape or the other, never both. Say “cheaper.” Never “more cheaper.” The count gives you one answer, not two.

The joining word is than, and only than. Say “lighter than my old one.” Never “lighter that my old one.”

Nothing is added between as and as. Say “as fast as that one.” Never “as faster as that one.”

The moves

Count the syllables, then say it. The frame does not change; only the blue words do.

one is more than the other

  • This one is [cheaper, quicker] than that one.
  • The blue one is [more expensive, more useful] than the black one.
  • My [keyboard, battery] is [better, worse] than yours.
  • This model is [safer, more modern] than the old one.

they are the same

  • This one is as [fast, simple] as that one.
  • The new model is as [safe, useful] as the old one.
  • It's not as [expensive, dangerous] as I thought.
  • There's no [difference, choice] between them.

Why this works

You compare things all day: two models, two offers, two ways of doing something. The form costs one count, and the word people actually get wrong is the small one at the end. Say than every time, and the second half of your sentence arrives.
