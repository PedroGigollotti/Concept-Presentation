ANALYSIS
------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal. Learn to say what you want to do. Then learn the four ways to say it.

THE STRUCTURE
After “like”, “love” and “hate”, the next verb takes “-ing”: “I like driving”. These three are ordinary verbs. For he and she they take unit 10's “-s”: “She likes driving”. Two endings in one short sentence.
   | like + -ing  ·  Form: person + like / love / hate + verb-ing  ·  Meaning: How you feel about doing it.  ·  Use it to: say which work you want  ·  example: I like driving.
   | the -s comes back  ·  Form: he / she + likes / loves / hates + verb-ing  ·  Meaning: The same, about somebody else.  ·  Use it to: say what a colleague wants  ·  example: She likes driving.
   | saying no  ·  Form: person + don't / doesn't like + verb-ing  ·  Meaning: Less than hate, and more usual.  ·  Use it to: turn work down politely  ·  example: I don't like filing.

WATCH OUT

   [ Use -ing, not the plain verb. Say “I like driving.” Never say “× I like drive.” This unit exists for that mistake. The -s is still there. Say “She likes driving.” Never say “× She like driving.” These are ordinary verbs, so unit 10's rule still runs. Hate is a strong word. At work, “I don't like filing” is what people say. “I hate filing” is true and it is loud. Both are correct; one of them ends the conversation. ]

   [ The same about a thing. These verbs also take a plain noun: “I like coffee”, “She hates Mondays”. The “-ing” is only for when the next word is a VERB. ]

HOW STRONG IT IS
Four ways to say it, and they are not the same answer.
   (figure) The verb after every one of these takes -ing. Only the place on the bar changes.

WHY THIS WORKS
At work this is not about your free time. It is how the work gets shared out. Who takes the calls, and who takes the reports. Say “I like drive” and everybody understands you. Mishear “hates” for “likes”. Then somebody spends a week on a job he hates.
