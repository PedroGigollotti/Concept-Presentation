ANALYSIS

Goal. Ask one question before you name a thing, and you will know whether to say the, to say a, or to say no word at all.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Do we both know which one?

Ask it about the other person, not about yourself. If the answer is yes, say the. If the other person does not know which one yet, say a or an. And when you mean every one of them, or you are using a name, put nothing in front at all.

The first time a, after that the

Priya says “I'm in a meeting with a client today”

One line later she says “It's in the big room” Same person, two words, one line between them.

The first time, Marco does not know there is a meeting or a client at all. So she opens with a. Once she has said it, they both know which meeting she means, and from then on it is the meeting.

So use a or an to bring a thing into the conversation, and the every time after that.

This works for anything: a printer → the printer. An elevator → the elevator.

the when only one of them is here

Sometimes you say the the very first time, and it is still right. Priya says “It's in the big room, on the second floor.” Neither of them has mentioned a floor before.

She can, because the building has one second floor. Marco knows which one she means before she finishes the sentence, and that is the only test there is.

Use it for anything there is one of where you are: the window, the floor, the kitchen, the manager, the meeting.

Ask the question and you will not get this wrong. If only one of them is here, we both know which one, and it is the.

Nothing in front of a name

A name has already said which one. So English puts no word in front of it at all.

Say “She's from Madrid.” Never “She's from the Madrid.”

The same goes for the name of a country, a city or a room: Madrid, Rome, room four. Priya says “on the second floor” with the, and “from Madrid” with nothing, in the same breath.

And nothing goes in front of my, your, his or her either. Say “my desk”, never “the my desk”.

Nothing in front when you mean all of them

This is the one your own language will fight you on, so read it twice.

Priya says “Clients from Madrid are always early.” with no word in front, and she means clients anywhere, all of them. One line earlier Marco says “And the client?”, and there he means one person the two of them already have in mind.

So drop the word when you mean every one of them: “Clients are always early.” Never “The clients are always early.”

Put it back the moment you narrow it down to some of them: “The clients from the Madrid office are always early.”

Watch out

No word in front of a name. Say “She's from Madrid.” Never “She's from the Madrid.” Your own language wants a word there. English will not have one.

No word when you mean every one of them. Say “Clients are always early.” Never “The clients are always early.” unless you mean some particular clients that you and the other person both have in mind.

Open with a, and only then move to the. Say “I'm in a meeting.” first. Never open with “I'm in the meeting” when nobody knows yet which meeting you mean.

American / British English

This book is American English. American English says an elevator; British English says a lift. Both are right, and everything this unit teaches is the same for either word.

American English also says a hallway where British English says a corridor. Use the American word here, and understand the British one when you hear it.

Why this works

This is the word you will say most often in English and the one your own language will keep putting in the wrong place. Both halves cost you something. Leave it out where English wants it and you sound vague. Put it in front of a name or in front of a whole trade and every listener hears it. The question is short and it settles all of it: do we both know which one? Ask it, and the word is chosen for you.
