31 · Verb complementation with meaning change

One verb, two very different meanings.

ACCESS

Goal. Read the exchange and notice the moves in bold. You are spotting the pattern here, not producing it yet.

Colleague Did you remember to send the invoice?

Priya I remember sending it, actually, last Tuesday. I also stopped to check the total before I sent it.

Colleague Good. Did you try reaching the client too?

Priya I tried calling twice, no luck. I stopped calling after the second attempt and emailed instead.

Colleague Makes sense, email might work better anyway.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how the same verb takes on a different meaning depending on whether it's followed by a gerund or an infinitive, and why the choice changes the whole sentence.

The structure

A small set of English verbs change meaning entirely depending on what follows them, which makes the choice between a gerund and an infinitive one of the highest-stakes grammar decisions in the language. Remember doing looks back at a completed memory; remember to do looks forward to a duty not yet fulfilled. Stop doing ends an activity; stop to do pauses one activity in order to start another. Getting the complement wrong doesn't just sound off, it reverses what actually happened.

Remember / forget

Form
remember/forget + verb-ing (past memory); remember/forget + to + verb (a duty)
Meaning
a memory of something already done, versus a duty not yet completed
Use it to
be precise about whether you're recalling the past or flagging a future obligation
I remember sending it last Tuesday. Did you remember to send the invoice?

Stop / try

Form
stop + verb-ing (end an activity); stop + to + verb (pause to start another); try + verb-ing (experiment); try + to + verb (attempt)
Meaning
ending an activity versus pausing for another; experimenting versus attempting something difficult
Use it to
distinguish ending something from pausing, or an experiment from a genuine attempt
I stopped calling after the second attempt. I stopped to check the total before I sent it.

Watch out

Don't swap stop doing and stop to do. “I stopped to smoke” means you paused another activity in order to smoke. “I stopped smoking” means you quit smoking entirely. These are opposite claims.

Don't confuse try doing with try to do. “Try restarting the server” suggests an experiment worth attempting to see what happens. Try to restart the server suggests a genuine, possibly difficult attempt. Match the verb to which meaning you intend.

American / British English.

Go on to do versus go on doing. Both varieties use go on to do something for moving to a new activity and go on doing something for continuing the same one, but American speech uses keep doing more often for the continuing sense. This book uses keep doing for continuation to avoid ambiguity.

The moves

Same verb, different meaning, in every line. The highlighted slot changes; the complement always decides which meaning applies.

  • I remember [sending it / seeing that email] last Tuesday.
  • Did you remember to [send the invoice / lock the door]?
  • I stopped [calling / emailing] after the second attempt.
  • I stopped to [check the total / confirm the details] before I sent it.
  • Try [restarting the server / clearing the cache] and see what happens.

Where each move sits

Same verb, opposite direction in time, depending on what follows it.

Why this works

These verbs are a small trap precisely because the sentence still sounds grammatical either way, so the listener has no signal that anything went wrong except the meaning itself quietly flipping. Getting the complement right means the listener can trust that the timeline you're describing is the one you actually mean, whether you're recalling something finished or flagging something still owed. Because the error is invisible at the surface level, it's worth deliberately checking these verbs whenever the stakes of the claim are high, like confirming whether an invoice was actually sent.
