32 · Phrasal verbs turned into nouns

Reports and summaries: turnout, fallout, buy-in, and the stress that gives them away.

ACCESS

Every phrasal verb you've learned can freeze into a noun, and those nouns run through every report and executive summary. There's a catch you can hear: the stress moves to the front. Listen for it as you read.

Two people drafting an executive summary for the board, compressing a whole quarter into a page.

Sol For the board summary: the rollout hit its numbers, but the fallout from the price change is real.

Mara Right. Lead with the turnout at the launch, then the breakdown by region.

Sol And we still don't have full buy-in from the sales team.

Mara Note it. Also flag the handover to the client's team next month.

Sol One more: engineering shipped a workaround, not a real fix. The board should know.

Mara Good. Every one of those is a phrasal verb wearing a noun's clothes. Say them out loud, the stress jumps to the front.

ANALYSIS

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Verb or noun — where is the stress?

Almost any phrasal verb can freeze into a noun: break down becomes a breakdown, roll out becomes a rollout. Two things change when it does, and both are mechanical: the stress moves to the front, and the spelling closes up.

The structure

This is the last move in the book, and the most quietly useful. Almost any phrasal verb can freeze into a noun. Break down becomes a breakdown; roll out becomes a rollout; hand over becomes a handover.

Two things change when the verb becomes a noun. First, the stress moves to the front. As a verb, the stress is on the particle: the system broke DOWN. As a noun, it jumps to the verb: a BREAKdown of costs. Second, the spelling tightens: most close up into one word, a few take a hyphen, and the meaning narrows to one specific sense.

The meaning narrows on the way

The stress and the spelling are the visible changes. The third change is the one worth watching, because nothing on the page announces it.

The verb break down covers a wide range: a machine stops, a person loses composure, a negotiation collapses. The noun a breakdown most often means one specific thing — an itemized list. The verb kept its range; the noun took one sense and kept it.

The same narrowing runs through the set. A handover is a specific event with a document; hand over is any transfer. When a phrasal verb freezes, it usually freezes around one meaning.

The test you can run with no book

Ask: is there an article in front of it?

A, the, this, or a possessive in front means you are looking at the noun, which means one word and the stress on the front. No article and it is the verb: two words, stress on the particle.

Say both out loud and the difference is unmistakable — broke DOWN against a BREAKdown. The stress is not decoration; it is how a listener knows which one you meant.

The mechanical half: a noun takes a plural, a verb takes a tense. Two rollouts, three handovers. If you can add an s for number, write it as one word.

Watch out. Not every phrasal verb makes a noun. You can hand over a project and write about the handover, but there is no a look after or a get up with. Don't invent them; check the noun is in real use.
American / British English. American English often compresses a phrasal verb into a single word: Americans downplay a risk where the British play it down. This book uses the two-word verb, and treats the closed form as a noun.

The phrasal-verb noun is compression. “We need agreement and active commitment from the sales team” becomes “we need sales buy-in.” A learner who says a break-DOWN of costs with the verb's stress marks themselves as non-native in one word; the one who says a BREAKdown sounds like they've written a hundred reports.
