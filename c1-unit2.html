2 · Continuous and perfect aspect for framing

Presenting a trend without overcommitting.

ACCESS

Goal. Read the update and notice the moves in bold. You are spotting the pattern here, not producing it yet.

Priya (portfolio manager) How's the fund performing?

Marcus (analyst) We've been seeing steady growth all quarter, so the curve looks healthy. We've seen a 12 percent lift since the rebalance.

Priya Is the risk exposure under control?

Marcus We've been working through it all week, and we've cleared the top ten positions as of today.

Priya Good. Keep me posted before the board call.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how continuous and perfect aspect split a trend into still-unfolding versus counted-and-closed, and how the choice keeps you from overcommitting.

The structure

Aspect, not tense, is doing the work here: both forms sit in the present perfect, but one views the activity as an unfinished stretch and the other views it as a completed whole. That difference matters when you report on a trend, because it lets you say exactly how settled the news is. We've been seeing growth keeps the trend open and still moving. We've seen a 12 percent lift closes it into a countable result.

Present perfect continuous

Form
have or has + been + verb-ing
Meaning
an activity in progress up to now, framed as ongoing and possibly still changing
Use it to
report a trend without committing to a final number or an end point
We’ve been seeing steady growth all quarter.

Present perfect simple

Form
have or has + past participle
Meaning
an action or state viewed as a complete whole up to now, often with a count or result
Use it to
report a trend as an established, measurable fact
We’ve seen a 12 percent increase since the relaunch.

Watch out

Don't put a number on the continuous. "We've been closing five deals" sounds wrong because a count needs a completed whole. Say "We've closed five deals."

Don't seal an ongoing situation with the simple. "We've solved the churn problem" claims it is finished. If it is still being worked on, say "We've been solving the churn problem."

American / British English.

Stative verbs in the continuous. Casual American marketing register sometimes stretches stative verbs into the continuous for energy: "We're loving this quarter's numbers." Standard and British usage keep these stative: "We love this quarter's numbers." This book uses the standard stative form.

The moves

Same aspect choice in every line. The highlighted slot changes; the aspect sets how settled the news sounds.

  • We've been seeing [steady growth / real traction] this quarter.
  • We've seen [a 12 percent lift / a clear uptick] since the rebalance.
  • We've been working through [the exposure / the portfolio review].
  • We've cleared [the backlog / the review] as of today.
  • So far, we've been trending [upward / in the right direction].

Where each move sits

Aspect is the dial. A counted result sits on the left; an activity still in motion sits on the right.

Why this works

A status report always makes an implicit promise about how solid the news is, and aspect is the tool that sets that promise without extra words. Store two ready frames: "we've done X" for a closed count, "we've been doing X" for a trend still in motion. Reach for the continuous when you want room to keep moving, or the raw number is not the point. Reach for the simple when a stakeholder needs a number to act on. Getting this wrong in either direction costs you: overcommitting with the simple invites a follow-up question you cannot answer, and hedging everything into the continuous makes you sound like you have nothing solid to report.
