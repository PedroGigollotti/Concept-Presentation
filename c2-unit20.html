20 · Collocation and near-synonyms

The word with the exact weight.

ACCESS

Dan (department head) How's she doing?

Farida (line manager) She's determined, not stubborn. She pushes till it's fixed.

ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn the conventional collocations and the loading of near-synonyms, so your word choice fits and takes the stance you intend. By the end you should be able to pick the word with the exact weight.

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

Which of these two words is the judgment I am willing to sign?

Near-synonyms describe the same behavior and grade it differently. In an appraisal the grade is the point, so the choice is the assessment.

The structure

Two related skills separate a precise C2 writer from a merely correct one: knowing which words go together, and knowing which of several close synonyms carries the exact weight you want. The first is collocation, the word partners English simply expects. You make a decision, never do a decision; rain is heavy, not strong; a result is highly likely, not strongly likely. There is often no logic to it, so you learn each partner along with the word. The second is close synonyms. Determined, persistent, and stubborn describe the same behavior, but the first praises, the second is neutral, and the third criticizes. Choosing among them is not just decoration: every choice quietly takes a side or sets a level of formality, whether you meant it to or not.

Collocation (fixed partnerships)

Form
verb + noun (make a decision, reach a conclusion), adj + noun (heavy rain, strong coffee), adv + adj (highly likely, bitterly disappointed)
Meaning
conventional combinations that sound right
Use it to
sound native; the wrong partner sounds off even when it's logical
make a decision (not do a decision); heavy rain (not strong rain); highly unlikely (not strongly unlikely)

Near-synonyms by connotation

Form
same literal meaning, different feeling: determined / persistent / stubborn; thrifty / careful / stingy; confident / cocky
Meaning
near-synonyms carry positive, neutral, or negative weight
Use it to
praise, stay neutral, or criticize with a single word
She's determined (positive) / persistent (neutral) / stubborn (negative), one trait, three stances

Near-synonyms by register

Form
general vs specific, formal vs informal: tell / inform / notify; get / obtain / procure; end / terminate
Meaning
the same rough meaning at different formalities and precisions
Use it to
match the word to the channel and the exact shade
We'll tell you (neutral) / notify you (formal, procedural) / let you know (casual)

Near-synonyms are not degrees of each other

Determined and stubborn describe the same person doing the same thing. What differs is whether the writer approves.

Thrifty and stingy, confident and arrogant, thorough and pedantic, flexible and inconsistent: each pair works the same way.

In an appraisal that means the word is the assessment. A manager who writes stubborn has graded the person, and no qualifier afterwards takes it back.

The test you can run with no book

Ask: would I say this word to their face, in these words?

If not, it is the wrong one for a written appraisal, which is read to their face by definition.

The mechanical half is Farida's move: when the pair is close, mark which side you mean. Thrifty, in the good sense. Careful, not stingy. One clause, and the reading is fixed.

Collocation, where no judgment is involved at all

Beside the near-synonyms sits a second thing with the same symptom: pairs that are simply fixed. Strong tea, heavy rain, a tight deadline, a steep increase, a sharp drop.

Powerful tea and strong workload are both perfectly logical and neither is English. Nothing underneath explains it and there is nothing to reason from.

The practical consequence is how you store vocabulary: not deadline but a tight deadline, not increase but a steep increase. A list of single words produces exactly these errors.

Why this is where fluent writing is judged

Everything in a well-built sentence can be correct and the pairing still off, and a reader registers it without being able to name it.

In an appraisal it is worse than a style problem: the near-synonym carries a verdict, so the wrong one records a judgment the manager did not intend and the employee will read.

This is the last layer of the language, and it is the one where a C2 speaker is separated from a very good C1 one.

Watch out

Collocations override logic. Powerful tea and strong workload are both logical, but only strong tea and heavy workload are right. You can't reason a collocation out; you learn the partner with the word.

Connotation is not optional. Choosing stubborn over determined passes a judgment, even if you only wanted a synonym. Know the loading of the word you pick, or you'll criticize when you meant to stay neutral, or the reverse.

American / British English.

Collocation differences. American make a reservation, British book a table; American fill out a form, British fill in a form. This book uses the American collocation, glossing where useful.

On the weekend. American on the weekend, British at the weekend. This book uses on the weekend.

The moves

Same idea, exact weight. Keep the frame; change only the slot and the situation.

  • Let's make a [decision / start] today.
  • That's highly [unlikely / effective].
  • She's [determined / persistent], not stubborn.
  • We'll [notify / inform] you formally.
  • It's a minor [setback / issue], not a crisis.

Where each move sits

One trait, three verdicts. Near-synonyms carry a judgment; the word you pick takes a side.

Why this works

Here is what these two skills give you. Collocations are the fingerprints of fluency: you cannot reason them out, only absorb them, so getting them right shows you have really lived in the language, while a single wrong pair, do a mistake, strong rain, gives a non-native ear away even in a flawless sentence. Close synonyms matter for a different reason: they are how English lets you pass judgment without seeming to. Call a plan ambitious and you have backed it; call it reckless and you have buried it; call it bold and you have admired the nerve while hinting at doubt, and you did all of that with one adjective, not an argument. The reader takes in the verdict without noticing they were handed one. So treat word choice as a decision about weight and fit, not just meaning.

1 · Under pressure

Near-synonyms are a judgment, and judgments slow you down, so this set is deliberately uncomfortable. Forty-five seconds each.

2 · Say it

A loaded word said warmly can pass as neutral, and a neutral word said coldly can read as criticism. The voice can override your word choice entirely.

Record situation 2. Play it back and listen for one thing: does a little inflexible sound gentle, or does it sound like you are being polite about something worse? Gentle criticism only works if the tone matches the hedge. If your voice is harder than your words, the hedge reads as sarcasm.

3 · With a partner, if you have one

take a single neutral trait (persistent, careful, confident, ambitious) and take turns naming it three ways, once approvingly, once neutrally, once critically (determined / persistent / stubborn). Your partner says which loading each word carried and whether it matched your intended tone. Then switch to collocations: one of you says a noun (decision, rain, deadline), the other supplies its correct partner (make, heavy, meet).

Then, find a description you wrote of a person or a plan, and check every adjective for its loading. Did you praise when you meant to stay neutral, or criticize by accident? Swap in the word whose weight matches what you actually meant.
