29 · Phrasal verbs in speech

Meetings, standups, and calls: where the phrasal verb is the expected choice.

ACCESS

This part of the book is about placement, not decoding. You know the verbs; the question now is when to reach for them. In speech, the phrasal verb is almost always the natural choice. Listen for the one line that sounds wrong, and ask why.

A morning standup. Most of the team speaks the way people actually speak. One new hire does not, yet.

Mara Quick standup, let's kick off. Where are we on the migration?

Sol We ran into a caching bug yesterday, but I worked around it. Back on track.

Mara Nice. Can you run the fix by security before it ships?

Priyanka I shall commence the review and subsequently transmit my observations.

Mara Ha, you can just say you'll go over it and get back to me. We keep it loose in here.

Priyanka Got it. I'll go over it and follow up by noon.

ANALYSIS

Everything you've learned so far decodes phrasal verbs. This last part of the book is the opposite skill: choosing to use them, and knowing where. Start with speech, because speech is where the phrasal verb is not just allowed but expected. In a standup, a call, or a hallway, the Latinate verb sounds stiff, distant, even a little pompous.

The reason is rhythm and relationship. Phrasal verbs are short, stressed, and Anglo-Saxon; they land in the ear the way spoken English is built to land. Let's kick off is three quick beats; let us commence is a lecture.

The left column is what people actually say. The right is grammatical, but out loud it sounds like a memo read aloud.

Watch out. Phrasal does not mean sloppy. Speech favors phrasal verbs, but clarity still rules. And over-correcting sounds worse than the error: a learner told formal writing is better often imports it into speech and sounds robotic.
American / British English. British English sorts out a problem; American English figures it out or works it out. All are spoken-natural. This book uses figure out and work out.

Hold one idea: in speech, shorter and stressed wins. Phrasal verbs are built from the oldest, most common words in English, the ones a listener processes without effort. The Latinate twin makes the listener translate, and translation, even for a fraction of a second, reads as distance.

E

Meet a Stranger

These three fill real meetings. If you don't know them, reason from the particle, then reveal the key.

1. “Let's take that offline and keep the standup short.”

2. “Can you loop in security before this ships?”

3. “Let's touch base after lunch on the numbers.”

take it offline: to move a side topic out of the group meeting. Offline: off the shared channel.

loop in: to bring someone into the conversation or decision. In: inside the loop.

touch base: to make brief contact to stay aligned. Base: a point you check in at.
