ANALYSIS

Goal. Learn how precise quantifiers let you scope a claim exactly, from a small handful to a genuine majority, without vague words like some or a lot.

The structure

Some and a lot are honest words, but they leave the listener guessing at the real scale of a problem or an opportunity. English has a much more precise set of quantifiers for exactly this purpose: a handful of signals a genuinely small number, a fair number of signals more than a few but not a majority, and few versus a few flips between an almost-none reading and a some-exist reading depending on a single word.

Precise quantity expressions

Form
a handful of / a fair number of / the vast majority of + plural noun
Meaning
a scaled, specific sense of quantity, from small to nearly all
Use it to
scope a claim precisely instead of relying on vague words like some or a lot
A fair number of accounts are affected. A handful of regions show the pattern.

Few / a few and neither / either

Form
few + plural noun (almost none); a few + plural noun (a small positive number); neither / either + of + the two + noun
Meaning
few is near-negative, a few is a small positive; neither and either scope a claim across exactly two items
Use it to
distinguish almost-none from some, and correctly scope a claim across two specific options
Few of them are high-value clients. Neither of the two largest markets is affected.

Watch out

Don't confuse few with a few. Few clients responded reads as almost none, disappointing. “A few clients responded” reads as a small number did, which may be perfectly fine. Choose based on which tone you mean.

Don't use neither or either for more than two items. Neither of the three markets is wrong; neither and either only work for exactly two. Use none of for three or more: none of the three markets.

American / British English.

A number of with verb agreement. Both varieties treat “a number of accounts are affected” as plural. “The number of accounts is rising” takes a singular verb in both. This book follows this standard rule consistently.

The moves

Same precision in every line. The highlighted slot changes; the quantifier always scopes the claim exactly.

  • A fair number of [accounts / tickets] are affected.
  • A handful of [regions / teams] show the pattern.
  • Few of [them / those clients] are high-value.
  • A few of [them / those clients] have already renewed.
  • Neither of the two [largest markets / biggest accounts] is affected.

Where each move sits

The same situation, now scoped with quantifiers precise enough to act on.

Why this works

A decision-maker reading a report needs to know the real scale of a problem before deciding how urgently to respond, and vague quantifiers force them to ask a follow-up question that precise ones would have already answered. A handful of and a fair number of each carry an implicit sense of scale, small versus moderate, that some simply doesn't. The single-word flip between few and a few is worth special attention precisely because it's so easy to miss, and getting it wrong can make a small win sound like a disappointment, or a real problem sound reassuringly minor.
