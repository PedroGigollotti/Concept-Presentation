ANALYSIS

ONE QUESTION RUNS THIS WHOLE UNIT

How far did you get, and how hard was it?

Get is the vaguest verb in English — to come to have, or come to be. Held still, every sharper meaning here is the particle measuring the journey: all the way, barely, around, or out.

The structure

The verb get is the busiest word in English precisely because it is so vague. On its own it just means to come to have, or come to be. Hold it still, and every sharper meaning here, survival, escape, progress, is the particle's doing.

Get is about motion toward a state, so its particles are about how far you make it and how hard it was. Get through it (all the way across, survive), get by (just past, barely), get around it (circle the obstacle), get on with it (keep moving), get back to someone (return), get out of it (escape).

Getting through, and getting by

These two sit closest together and carry very different news about you.

Get through the quarter and you came out the other side: it is over. Get by and nothing is over — you are still in it, and only just. One is a finish line, the other is a margin.

That is why get by almost always attracts a phrase saying on how little: getting by on one analyst, on a smaller budget, on four hours. The particle means barely past, and the sentence usually wants to say past what.

The test you can run with no book

Ask: how much of the distance did you cover?

All of it, and the particle is through. Barely any margin, and it is by. None of it, because you went round, and it is around. Past the thing and in front of it, and it is ahead.

Because get contributes almost nothing, this unit is the best place in the book to practise reading a phrasal verb backwards: cover the verb, look only at the particle, and the sense is still there.

The mechanical half: get by takes no object. You get through something; you simply get by. The moment an object appears, the particle has to change.

Watch out. Get through has three senses: survive the day, finish the work, or reach someone by phone or understanding. And get on has two lives: continue a task, or have a good relationship with a person.
American / British English. For relationships, British English says they get on; American English says they get along. This book uses get along for people and get on with for tasks.

Because get is nearly meaningless alone, it is the clearest proof the method has. Get through and get out of share a verb and could not be further apart: one is survival, the other is escape, and the entire difference is through versus out of.

Your prediction: get ahead. You predicted this one from ahead before you read any of the unit: you move in front of the thing instead of following it. Across this unit get is the verb of changing position or state, and the particle says which way.
