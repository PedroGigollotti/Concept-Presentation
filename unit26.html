26 · GET across the particles

Progress and difficulty: how far you make it, and how hard it was.

ACCESS

Another prediction clinic. The verb get never changes; only the particle moves. One phrase, get ahead, is never taught here. Predict it from the particle before you read on.

A support and operations team in the middle of the month's third outage.

Rosa Third outage this month. We need to get through today without losing the big accounts.

Tomas We're getting by on the failover, but it won't hold under peak load.

Rosa Then get around the bottleneck manually while we patch it.

Tomas On it. Once it's stable, I'll get on with the root-cause writeup.

Rosa Good. If we fix the caching, we finally get ahead of the incidents instead of chasing them.

Tomas Agreed. I'll get back to the enterprise clients the moment we're green.

ANALYSIS

The verb get is the busiest word in English precisely because it is so vague. On its own it just means to come to have, or come to be. Hold it still, and every sharper meaning here, survival, escape, progress, is the particle's doing.

Get is about motion toward a state, so its particles are about how far you make it and how hard it was. Get through it (all the way across, survive), get by (just past, barely), get around it (circle the obstacle), get on with it (keep moving), get back to someone (return), get out of it (escape).

Read down the particle column. You already know each one. Predict the meaning before your eye reaches the third column.

Watch out. Get through has three senses: survive the day, finish the work, or reach someone by phone or understanding. And get on has two lives: continue a task, or have a good relationship with a person.
American / British English. For relationships, British English says they get on; American English says they get along. This book uses get along for people and get on with for tasks.

Because get is nearly meaningless alone, it is the clearest proof the method has. Get through and get out of share a verb and could not be further apart: one is survival, the other is escape, and the entire difference is through versus out of.

get along — to manage adequately, or to have a good relationship. Along: onward together.

get at — to imply, or to reach. At: aiming toward a point.

get away with — to escape the consequences of something. Away with: carrying it off out of reach.
